## Part A: Rings

### A1. Ideals and quotients (revision)

An *ideal* $I \subseteq R$ is an additive subgroup with $rI \subseteq I$ for all $r \in R$. The quotient $R/I$ is a ring, and the isomorphism theorems hold as for groups: if $\varphi: R \to S$ is a homomorphism, $R/\ker\varphi \cong \operatorname{im}\varphi$.

- $P$ is *prime* if $P \ne R$ and $ab \in P \Rightarrow a\in P$ or $b \in P$. Equivalently, $R/P$ is an integral domain.
- $M$ is *maximal* if $M\ne R$ and no ideal lies strictly between $M$ and $R$. Equivalently, $R/M$ is a field. Maximal ⇒ prime.
- By Zorn's lemma, every proper ideal is contained in a maximal ideal.
- **Chinese remainder theorem.** If $I_1,\dots,I_k$ are pairwise *comaximal* ($I_i + I_j = R$), then $R/(I_1\cap\dots\cap I_k) \cong \prod R/I_j$. For $R = \mathbb Z$: $\mathbb Z/mn \cong \mathbb Z/m \times \mathbb Z/n$ when $\gcd(m,n) = 1$.

### A2. Euclidean domains, PIDs, UFDs

Let $R$ be an integral domain.

- $R$ is a *Euclidean domain* if there is $N : R\setminus\lbrace0\rbrace \to \mathbb Z_{\ge0}$ such that for all $a$ and $b\ne0$ there are $q, r$ with $a = qb + r$ and $r = 0$ or $N(r) < N(b)$. Examples: $\mathbb Z$, $F[x]$, $\mathbb Z[i]$ with $N(a+bi) = a^2+b^2$.
- $R$ is a *principal ideal domain (PID)* if every ideal is $(a)$ for some $a$.
- $R$ is a *unique factorisation domain (UFD)* if every non-zero non-unit is a product of irreducibles, uniquely up to order and units.

**Theorem.** Euclidean ⇒ PID ⇒ UFD.

*Euclidean ⇒ PID:* a non-zero ideal $I$ is generated by an element of minimal norm (the remainder of any element on division by it lies in $I$ and has smaller norm, so is $0$).

*PID ⇒ UFD (sketch):* (i) In a PID, irreducible ⇒ prime: if $p$ is irreducible, $(p)$ is maximal among principal ideals, hence maximal, hence prime. (ii) Existence of factorisations: a PID is *Noetherian* (an ascending chain of ideals $(a_1)\subseteq(a_2)\subseteq\cdots$ has union an ideal $(a)$, and $a$ lies in some $(a_n)$, so the chain stabilises); if some element had no factorisation, repeatedly splitting it would give a strictly ascending chain. (iii) Uniqueness follows from "irreducible ⇒ prime" exactly as in $\mathbb Z$.

**Counterexamples.**

- $\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]$ is not a UFD: $6 = 2\cdot 3 = (1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$, and all four factors are irreducible and non-associate (use the norm $a^2+5b^2$: there is no element of norm $2$ or $3$).
- $\mathbb Z[x]$ and $F[x,y]$ are UFDs but not PIDs: $(2,x)$ and $(x,y)$ are not principal.
- $\mathbb Z[(1+\sqrt{-19})/2]$ is a PID but not Euclidean (statement; see DF 8.2).

In a PID, $\gcd(a,b)$ exists and generates $(a,b)$, so it can be written $ra+sb$ (Bézout).

### A3. Polynomial rings and irreducibility

**Gauss's lemma.** Let $R$ be a UFD with fraction field $F$. If $f \in R[x]$ is reducible in $F[x]$, it is reducible in $R[x]$ (into factors of the same degrees as in $F[x]$, after rescaling). A primitive polynomial (coefficients with gcd 1) is irreducible in $R[x]$ iff it is irreducible in $F[x]$. Consequence: **if $R$ is a UFD, so is $R[x]$.** Hence $\mathbb Z[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ and $F[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ are UFDs.

**Tests for irreducibility over $\mathbb Q$.**

1. *Degree 2 or 3:* irreducible iff no root. Use the rational root test: a root $p/q$ in lowest terms of $a_nx^n + \dots + a_0 \in \mathbb Z[x]$ has $p \mid a_0$, $q \mid a_n$.
2. *Eisenstein:* if a prime $p$ divides $a_0,\dots,a_{n-1}$, $p \nmid a_n$ and $p^2 \nmid a_0$, then $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$. Often applied after a substitution $x \mapsto x + c$: e.g. $\Phi_p(x) = \frac{x^p-1}{x-1}$ becomes Eisenstein at $p$ after $x\mapsto x+1$.
3. *Reduction mod $p$:* if $p \nmid a_n$ and $\bar f \in \mathbb F_p[x]$ is irreducible, then $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$. (The converse fails: $x^4+1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ but reducible mod every prime.)

## Part B: Modules

### B1. Basics

An $R$-*module* is an abelian group $M$ with a scalar multiplication $R \times M \to M$ satisfying the vector space axioms. Examples:

- $R$-modules for $R = F$ a field are vector spaces.
- $\mathbb Z$-modules are exactly abelian groups.
- An $F[x]$-module is a vector space $V$ together with a linear map $T : V\to V$ (where $x$ acts as $T$). Submodules are $T$-invariant subspaces. This dictionary is how module theory proves canonical form theorems.
- Ideals of $R$ are the submodules of $R$.

Submodules, quotients, homomorphisms, kernels, and the isomorphism theorems work as for groups. The *direct sum* $M \oplus N$ is the product with componentwise operations.

A module is *free* if it has a *basis* (a linearly independent spanning set); then $M \cong R^{(I)}$. For commutative $R$, all bases of a free module have the same cardinality (the *rank*). Over a non-field, many modules are not free: $\mathbb Z/2$ is not a free $\mathbb Z$-module, and neither is $\mathbb Q$.

$m \in M$ is a *torsion* element if $rm = 0$ for some non-zero $r \in R$. Over a domain, the torsion elements form a submodule $\operatorname{Tor}(M)$.

**Pitfalls (compared with vector spaces).** A linearly independent set need not extend to a basis ($\lbrace 2\rbrace$ in $\mathbb Z$); a spanning set need not contain a basis ($\lbrace 2,3\rbrace$ spans $\mathbb Z$); a submodule need not have a complement ($2\mathbb Z \subseteq \mathbb Z$); a submodule of a finitely generated module need not be finitely generated (over non-Noetherian rings).

### B2. Submodules of free modules over a PID

**Theorem.** Let $R$ be a PID. Every submodule $N$ of $R^n$ is free of rank $\le n$.

*Proof.* Induction on $n$. For $n = 1$, $N$ is an ideal $(a)$, which is $0$ or free on $a$ (as $R$ is a domain). For $n > 1$, let $\pi : R^n \to R$ be the last coordinate. Then $\pi(N)$ is an ideal $(a)$, and $\ker(\pi|_N) = N \cap R^{n-1}$ is free of rank $\le n-1$ by induction. If $a = 0$, done. Otherwise pick $y\in N$ with $\pi(y) = a$; then $N = (N\cap R^{n-1}) \oplus Ry$, so $N$ is free of rank $\le n$. $\square$

### B3. Smith normal form and the structure theorem

**Smith normal form.** Let $R$ be a PID (for computation, think Euclidean) and $A$ an $m\times n$ matrix over $R$. There are invertible matrices $P \in GL_m(R)$, $Q\in GL_n(R)$ with
$$
PAQ = \operatorname{diag}(d_1, d_2, \dots, d_r, 0, \dots, 0), \qquad d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_r, \quad d_i \ne 0.
$$
The $d_i$ are unique up to units: $d_1\cdots d_k$ is the gcd of the $k\times k$ minors of $A$.

*Algorithm (Euclidean case).* Using row and column swaps, adding multiples of one row (column) to another, and multiplying by units: move an entry of minimal norm to position $(1,1)$; use division with remainder to clear the first row and column; if a remainder is non-zero it has smaller norm, so move it to $(1,1)$ and repeat. Once row and column 1 are clear, if $d_1$ does not divide some entry $b$, add that row to row 1 and repeat (this lowers the norm of the $(1,1)$ entry). Then recurse on the lower-right block.

**Structure theorem (existence).** Let $R$ be a PID and $M$ a finitely generated $R$-module. Then
$$
M \cong R^r \oplus R/(d_1) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(d_k), \qquad d_1 \mid d_2 \mid\cdots\mid d_k, \ d_i \text{ non-zero non-units}.
$$
*Proof.* Choose generators $m_1,\dots,m_n$, giving a surjection $\varphi: R^n \to M$. By B2, $\ker\varphi$ is free of rank $s\le n$; choosing a basis gives an $n\times s$ matrix $A$ whose columns span $\ker\varphi$, so $M \cong R^n/AR^s$. Changing bases of $R^n$ and $R^s$ replaces $A$ by $PAQ$, so we may assume $A$ is in Smith normal form. Then $R^n/AR^s \cong \bigoplus_i R/(d_i) \oplus R^{n-s}$. Drop factors $R/(d_i)$ with $d_i$ a unit (they are zero). $\square$

**Uniqueness.** The rank $r$ and the ideals $(d_i)$ (the *invariant factors*) are uniquely determined by $M$. (One proof: the rank is $\dim_F (F\otimes M)$ for $F$ the fraction field; for the torsion part, the number of $d_i$ divisible by a power $p^j$ is read off from $\dim_{R/(p)}\, p^{j-1}M/p^jM$.)

**Elementary divisor form.** By the Chinese remainder theorem, each $R/(d)$ with $d = u\,p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_t^{a_t}$ splits as $\bigoplus R/(p_i^{a_i})$. So
$$
M \cong R^r \oplus \bigoplus_{i} R/(p_i^{a_i}),
$$
with the prime powers $p_i^{a_i}$ (the *elementary divisors*) unique up to order and units. To convert back: for each prime, list its powers in decreasing order; the largest invariant factor is the product of the largest power of each prime, and so on.

### B4. Application 1: finitely generated abelian groups

With $R = \mathbb Z$: every finitely generated abelian group is $\mathbb Z^r \oplus \mathbb Z/d_1 \oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb Z/d_k$ with $1 < d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_k$, uniquely. The number of abelian groups of order $p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_t^{a_t}$ is $\prod p(a_i)$, where $p(a)$ is the number of partitions of $a$.

*Example.* Order $72 = 2^3\cdot 3^2$: $p(3)p(2) = 3\cdot2 = 6$ groups.

*Example.* If $G = \mathbb Z^3/A\mathbb Z^3$ and $A$ has Smith normal form $\operatorname{diag}(2,6,12)$, then $G \cong \mathbb Z/2\oplus\mathbb Z/6\oplus\mathbb Z/12$, of order $|\det A| = 144$.

### B5. Application 2: canonical forms

Let $V$ be an $n$-dimensional vector space over $F$ and $T : V\to V$ linear. Make $V$ an $F[x]$-module via $T$. It is finitely generated and torsion (by Cayley–Hamilton, or simply because $V$ is finite-dimensional so $I, T, T^2,\dots$ are dependent). So
$$
V \cong F[x]/(a_1) \oplus\cdots\oplus F[x]/(a_k), \qquad a_1\mid\cdots\mid a_k, \ a_i \text{ monic of degree} \ge 1.
$$
- On $F[x]/(a)$ with $a = x^d + c_{d-1}x^{d-1} + \dots + c_0$, multiplication by $x$ in the basis $1, x, \dots, x^{d-1}$ is the **companion matrix** $C(a)$ (ones on the subdiagonal, last column $-c_0,\dots,-c_{d-1}$).
- **Rational canonical form:** $T$ has matrix $C(a_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus C(a_k)$. It exists over any field and is unique.
- The **minimal polynomial** of $T$ is $a_k$; the **characteristic polynomial** is $a_1\cdots a_k$. This proves Cayley–Hamilton: $m_T \mid \chi_T$.
- **Jordan canonical form:** if $\chi_T$ splits over $F$ (e.g. $F = \mathbb C$), the elementary divisors are $(x-\lambda)^e$, and on $F[x]/((x-\lambda)^e)$ with basis $(x-\lambda)^{e-1},\dots,(x-\lambda),1$ the matrix is a Jordan block $J_e(\lambda)$. So $T$ is similar to a direct sum of Jordan blocks, unique up to order.
- Two matrices are similar iff they have the same invariant factors (equivalently, same rational canonical form). They are similar over $F$ iff they are similar over any extension field $K\supseteq F$ (invariant factors can be computed by Smith normal form of $xI - A$ over $F[x]$, which does not change when extending the field).

**Reading off Jordan form data.** For eigenvalue $\lambda$:
- the exponent of $(x-\lambda)$ in $\chi_T$ = total size of the $\lambda$-blocks (algebraic multiplicity);
- the exponent in $m_T$ = size of the largest $\lambda$-block;
- $\dim\ker(T-\lambda)$ = number of $\lambda$-blocks (geometric multiplicity);
- $\dim\ker(T-\lambda)^j - \dim\ker(T-\lambda)^{j-1}$ = number of $\lambda$-blocks of size $\ge j$.

$T$ is diagonalisable iff $m_T$ splits with distinct roots.

## Part C: Fields

### C1. Extensions and degree

A *field extension* $K/F$ is a field $K$ containing $F$. $K$ is an $F$-vector space; its dimension $[K:F]$ is the *degree*.

**Tower law.** If $F \subseteq K\subseteq L$, then $[L:F] = [L:K][K:F]$ (if $\lbrace \alpha_i\rbrace$ is a basis of $K/F$ and $\lbrace\beta_j\rbrace$ of $L/K$, then $\lbrace\alpha_i\beta_j\rbrace$ is a basis of $L/F$).

$\alpha \in K$ is *algebraic* over $F$ if $f(\alpha) = 0$ for some non-zero $f\in F[x]$; otherwise *transcendental*. The *minimal polynomial* $m_\alpha$ is the monic generator of the kernel of $F[x]\to K$, $f\mapsto f(\alpha)$. It is irreducible, and
$$
F(\alpha) = F[\alpha] \cong F[x]/(m_\alpha), \qquad [F(\alpha):F] = \deg m_\alpha.
$$
A basis is $1, \alpha, \dots, \alpha^{d-1}$. Conversely, for any irreducible $f\in F[x]$, $F[x]/(f)$ is a field containing a root of $f$ (**Kronecker's construction**).

- $[K:F] < \infty$ ⇒ $K/F$ algebraic. $F(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ with each $\alpha_i$ algebraic is finite. Algebraic over algebraic is algebraic. The algebraic elements of $K$ over $F$ form a subfield.
- Example: $\overline{\mathbb Q}$ (algebraic numbers in $\mathbb C$) is algebraic over $\mathbb Q$ of infinite degree.

**Ruler-and-compass constructions.** If $\alpha\in\mathbb R$ is constructible from $0,1$, then $\alpha$ lies in a tower $\mathbb Q = F_0\subseteq F_1\subseteq\cdots\subseteq F_k$ with $[F_{i}:F_{i-1}] = 2$, so $[\mathbb Q(\alpha):\mathbb Q]$ is a power of $2$. Hence: $\sqrt[3]{2}$ is not constructible (no duplicating the cube); $\cos 20^\circ$ has degree 3 (no trisecting $60^\circ$); $\pi$ is transcendental (Lindemann), so the circle cannot be squared. The regular $n$-gon is constructible iff $\varphi(n)$ is a power of $2$ (Gauss–Wantzel; the "if" direction uses Galois theory).

### C2. Splitting fields and algebraic closure

A *splitting field* of $f\in F[x]$ is an extension $K$ in which $f$ factors into linear factors and which is generated by the roots of $f$.

**Theorem.** Splitting fields exist (adjoin roots one at a time using Kronecker's construction), $[K:F]\le n!$ for $\deg f = n$, and any two splitting fields of $f$ over $F$ are isomorphic over $F$.

The uniqueness rests on the **extension lemma**: if $\sigma : F\to F'$ is an isomorphism, $f\in F[x]$, $K$ a splitting field of $f$ and $K'$ a splitting field of $\sigma f$, then $\sigma$ extends to an isomorphism $K\to K'$. The number of extensions is at most $[K:F]$, with equality iff every irreducible factor of $f$ has distinct roots (separability). *Proof idea:* if $\alpha$ is a root of an irreducible factor $g$ of $f$, then $\sigma$ extends to $F(\alpha) \cong F[x]/(g) \to F'[x]/(\sigma g)\cong F'(\alpha')$ for each root $\alpha'$ of $\sigma g$ in $K'$; induct on $[K:F]$.

*Examples.* $x^3-2$ over $\mathbb Q$: splitting field $\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2,\omega)$, $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$, degree $6$. $x^p - 1$: splitting field $\mathbb Q(\zeta_p)$, degree $p-1$. $x^4 - 2$: $\mathbb Q(\sqrt[4]2, i)$, degree 8.

**Algebraic closure.** A field $\Omega$ is *algebraically closed* if every non-constant polynomial in $\Omega[x]$ has a root in $\Omega$. An *algebraic closure* $\overline F$ of $F$ is an algebraic extension that is algebraically closed. Every field has an algebraic closure, unique up to (non-unique) isomorphism over $F$ (Zorn's lemma; Milne Ch. 6). $\mathbb C$ is algebraically closed (fundamental theorem of algebra; see module 2.2), so $\overline{\mathbb Q}$ = algebraic numbers in $\mathbb C$.

### C3. Separability

$f\in F[x]$ is *separable* if it has distinct roots in a splitting field. **Test:** $f$ is separable iff $\gcd(f, f') = 1$.

- If $f$ is irreducible, then $\gcd(f,f')$ is $1$ or $f$; the latter happens iff $f' = 0$. In characteristic $0$, $f'=0$ only for constants, so **every irreducible polynomial is separable**. In characteristic $p$, $f' = 0$ iff $f(x) = g(x^p)$.
- $F$ is *perfect* if every irreducible polynomial over $F$ is separable. Fields of characteristic $0$ and finite fields are perfect. (Over $\mathbb F_q$, $g(x^p) = h(x)^p$ by surjectivity of Frobenius.)
- Non-example: over $F = \mathbb F_p(t)$, $x^p - t$ is irreducible (Eisenstein at the prime $t$ of $\mathbb F_p[t]$) but equals $(x - t^{1/p})^p$ in its splitting field.

An algebraic extension $K/F$ is *separable* if the minimal polynomial of every element is separable.

**Primitive element theorem.** A finite separable extension $K/F$ is simple: $K = F(\gamma)$ for some $\gamma$. (For infinite $F$: show $F(\alpha,\beta) = F(\alpha + c\beta)$ for all but finitely many $c\in F$. For finite $F$: $K^\times$ is cyclic.) Example: $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2,\sqrt3) = \mathbb Q(\sqrt2+\sqrt3)$.

### C4. Finite fields

Let $F$ be a finite field. Its prime subfield is $\mathbb F_p$ for $p = \operatorname{char}F$, so $|F| = p^n$ where $n = [F:\mathbb F_p]$.

**Theorem.**
1. For each prime power $q = p^n$ there is a field $\mathbb F_q$ with $q$ elements: the splitting field of $x^q - x$ over $\mathbb F_p$, which consists exactly of the roots of $x^q - x$ (they form a field because $(a+b)^p = a^p + b^p$, and they are distinct because $(x^q-x)' = -1$).
2. Any field with $q$ elements is a splitting field of $x^q-x$ (each element satisfies $a^q = a$ by Lagrange on $F^\times$), hence isomorphic to $\mathbb F_q$.
3. $\mathbb F_q^\times$ is cyclic. (A finite subgroup of the multiplicative group of any field is cyclic.)
4. $\mathbb F_{p^m}\subseteq\mathbb F_{p^n}$ iff $m\mid n$, and then the subfield is unique.
5. $\mathbb F_{p^n}/\mathbb F_p$ is Galois with cyclic group of order $n$ generated by the **Frobenius** $\phi(a) = a^p$.
6. $x^{p^n} - x = \prod$ (all monic irreducible polynomials over $\mathbb F_p$ of degree dividing $n$). Counting degrees gives $p^n = \sum_{d\mid n} d\,N_d$, where $N_d$ = number of monic irreducibles of degree $d$.

## Part D: Galois theory

### D1. Galois extensions

For an extension $K/F$, $\operatorname{Aut}(K/F)$ is the group of field automorphisms of $K$ fixing $F$ pointwise. For a group $H$ of automorphisms of $K$, $K^H$ is the fixed field.

**Basic bound.** If $K/F$ is finite, $|\operatorname{Aut}(K/F)|\le[K:F]$.

**Definition/Theorem.** For a finite extension $K/F$ the following are equivalent; if they hold, $K/F$ is *Galois* and $\operatorname{Gal}(K/F) := \operatorname{Aut}(K/F)$:

1. $|\operatorname{Aut}(K/F)| = [K:F]$;
2. $F = K^{\operatorname{Aut}(K/F)}$;
3. $K$ is the splitting field of a separable polynomial over $F$;
4. $K/F$ is **normal** (every irreducible polynomial in $F[x]$ with a root in $K$ splits in $K$) and separable.

**Artin's theorem.** If $G$ is a finite group of automorphisms of a field $K$, then $[K:K^G] = |G|$ and $K/K^G$ is Galois with group $G$.

*Proof idea.* For $\alpha\in K$, the polynomial $\prod_{\beta\in G\alpha}(x - \beta)$ is fixed by $G$, so has coefficients in $K^G$; it is separable of degree $\le |G|$. So every element of $K$ has degree $\le |G|$ over $K^G$, and $K/K^G$ is separable; by the primitive element theorem, $[K:K^G]\le|G|$. Combined with $|G|\le|\operatorname{Aut}(K/K^G)|\le[K:K^G]$, equality holds.

**Examples.**
- $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)/\mathbb Q$: Galois, group $\mathbb Z/2$.
- $\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2)/\mathbb Q$: **not** Galois. Its only automorphism is the identity, because the other roots of $x^3-2$ are not real. Not normal.
- $\mathbb F_p(t)/\mathbb F_p(t^p)$: not Galois (not separable).

### D2. The fundamental theorem

**Fundamental theorem of Galois theory.** Let $K/F$ be finite Galois with group $G$. Then
$$
H \longmapsto K^H, \qquad E \longmapsto \operatorname{Gal}(K/E)
$$
are mutually inverse inclusion-reversing bijections between subgroups $H\le G$ and intermediate fields $F\subseteq E\subseteq K$. Moreover:

1. $[K:K^H] = |H|$ and $[K^H:F] = [G:H]$.
2. $K/E$ is always Galois. $E/F$ is Galois iff $\operatorname{Gal}(K/E)$ is normal in $G$, and then $\operatorname{Gal}(E/F)\cong G/\operatorname{Gal}(K/E)$ (by restriction).
3. For $\sigma\in G$, $\sigma(K^H) = K^{\sigma H\sigma^{-1}}$: conjugate subgroups ↔ conjugate fields.
4. Joins and meets correspond: $K^{H_1\cap H_2} = K^{H_1}K^{H_2}$ (compositum), $K^{\langle H_1,H_2\rangle} = K^{H_1}\cap K^{H_2}$.

*Proof sketch.* $K/E$ is Galois because $K$ is the splitting field over $E$ of the same separable polynomial. So $E = K^{\operatorname{Gal}(K/E)}$. Artin gives $\operatorname{Gal}(K/K^H) = H$ (since $|\operatorname{Gal}(K/K^H)| = [K:K^H] = |H|$ and it contains $H$). So the maps are inverse. For (2): $\sigma(E) = E$ for all $\sigma$ iff $\operatorname{Gal}(K/E)$ is normal (by 3); then restriction $G\to\operatorname{Aut}(E/F)$ has kernel $\operatorname{Gal}(K/E)$, and counting gives the isomorphism.

**Worked example: $x^3 - 2$ over $\mathbb Q$.** $K = \mathbb Q(\alpha,\omega)$ with $\alpha = \sqrt[3]2$, $\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$, $[K:\mathbb Q] = 6$. $G$ acts faithfully on the three roots $\alpha,\alpha\omega,\alpha\omega^2$, so $G\le S_3$; with order 6, $G \cong S_3$. Subgroups and fixed fields:

| Subgroup | Order | Fixed field | Normal? |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1$ | 1 | $K$ | yes |
| $\langle\tau\rangle$, $\tau$ = complex conjugation (fixes $\alpha$) | 2 | $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$ | no |
| transposition fixing $\alpha\omega$ | 2 | $\mathbb Q(\alpha\omega)$ | no |
| transposition fixing $\alpha\omega^2$ | 2 | $\mathbb Q(\alpha\omega^2)$ | no |
| $A_3 = \langle\sigma\rangle$, $\sigma:\alpha\mapsto\alpha\omega,\ \omega\mapsto\omega$ | 3 | $\mathbb Q(\omega) = \mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})$ | yes |
| $S_3$ | 6 | $\mathbb Q$ | yes |

**Worked example: cyclotomic fields.** $\Phi_n(x) = \prod_{\gcd(k,n)=1}(x - \zeta_n^k)$ has integer coefficients and is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ (for $n=p$ prime: Eisenstein after $x\mapsto x+1$; general $n$: DF 13.6). $\mathbb Q(\zeta_n)$ is the splitting field of $x^n - 1$, and $\sigma_a: \zeta_n\mapsto\zeta_n^a$ gives
$$
\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\zeta_n)/\mathbb Q)\cong(\mathbb Z/n)^\times,
$$
an abelian group. Every subfield is Galois over $\mathbb Q$. (Kronecker–Weber, statement: every abelian extension of $\mathbb Q$ lies in some $\mathbb Q(\zeta_n)$.)

**Galois groups of polynomials.** $\operatorname{Gal}(f)$ acts faithfully on the $n$ roots, so embeds in $S_n$; it is transitive iff $f$ is irreducible (for separable $f$). With the discriminant $\Delta = \prod_{i<j}(\alpha_i - \alpha_j)^2 \in F$: $\operatorname{Gal}(f)\le A_n$ iff $\sqrt\Delta\in F$ (char $\ne 2$). For an irreducible cubic: $\operatorname{Gal} = A_3$ if $\Delta$ is a square, else $S_3$. For $x^3 + px + q$, $\Delta = -4p^3 - 27q^2$.

### D3. Solvability by radicals

A group $G$ is *solvable* if it has a chain $1 = G_0 \trianglelefteq G_1\trianglelefteq\cdots\trianglelefteq G_k = G$ with each $G_{i}/G_{i-1}$ abelian. Subgroups and quotients of solvable groups are solvable, and an extension of solvable by solvable is solvable. $S_n$ is solvable for $n\le4$, and not for $n\ge5$ because $A_n$ is simple and non-abelian for $n \ge 5$.

A finite extension $K/F$ is *radical* if there is a tower $F = K_0\subseteq K_1\subseteq\cdots\subseteq K_m \supseteq K$ with $K_{i} = K_{i-1}(\alpha_i)$ and $\alpha_i^{n_i}\in K_{i-1}$. $f$ is *solvable by radicals* if its splitting field lies in a radical extension.

**Key ingredients (characteristic 0).**
1. If $F$ contains a primitive $n$-th root of unity and $a\in F$, then $F(\sqrt[n]a)/F$ is Galois with cyclic group (of order dividing $n$). (**Kummer**; Problem 22.)
2. $F(\zeta_n)/F$ is Galois with abelian group (a subgroup of $(\mathbb Z/n)^\times$).
3. Conversely (Kummer, with Hilbert 90): if $F$ contains a primitive $n$-th root of unity and $K/F$ is cyclic of degree $n$, then $K = F(\sqrt[n]a)$.

**Theorem (Galois).** In characteristic 0, $f\in F[x]$ is solvable by radicals iff $\operatorname{Gal}(f)$ is solvable.

*Proof of "⇒" (sketch).* Enlarge the radical tower: adjoin a primitive $N$-th root of unity first ($N$ = product of the $n_i$), and take the Galois closure (still radical). Each step is then abelian (by 1 and 2), so the Galois group of the top field over $F$ is solvable. $\operatorname{Gal}(f)$ is a quotient of it, hence solvable.

**Insolubility of the quintic.** Let $f\in\mathbb Q[x]$ be irreducible of prime degree $p$ with exactly $p-2$ real roots. Then $\operatorname{Gal}(f)\cong S_p$.

*Proof.* $p$ divides $|G|$ (since $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$ has degree $p$ and lies in the splitting field), so by Cauchy $G$ contains an element of order $p$, which in $S_p$ is a $p$-cycle. Complex conjugation restricts to an element of $G$ swapping the two non-real roots and fixing the others: a transposition. A $p$-cycle and a transposition generate $S_p$ ($p$ prime). $\square$

*Example:* $x^5 - 6x + 3$ (Eisenstein at 3; three real roots by the intermediate value theorem and Rolle). Its Galois group $S_5$ is not solvable, so its roots cannot be expressed by radicals. Note that the general quintic $x^5 + a_4x^4+\dots+a_0$ over $\mathbb Q(a_0,\dots,a_4)$ has group $S_5$ too (Abel–Ruffini).

## Common pitfalls

1. "Irreducible" and "prime" coincide in UFDs but not in general ($2$ in $\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]$ is irreducible, not prime).
2. A polynomial can be irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ but have a reducible reduction mod every $p$ ($x^4+1$). Reduction mod $p$ only proves irreducibility, never reducibility.
3. $[F(\alpha,\beta):F]$ is at most $[F(\alpha):F]\,[F(\beta):F]$ but need not equal it: $\alpha = \sqrt[3]2$, $\beta = \omega\sqrt[3]2$ give $\mathbb Q(\alpha,\beta) = \mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]2,\omega)$ of degree 6, not 9. (Equality does hold when the two degrees are coprime.)
4. $\operatorname{Aut}(K/F)$ is always defined, but the Galois correspondence needs $K/F$ Galois.
5. Normality is not transitive: $\mathbb Q\subseteq\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)\subseteq\mathbb Q(\sqrt[4]2)$, each step degree 2 (hence normal), but $\mathbb Q(\sqrt[4]2)/\mathbb Q$ is not normal.
6. Invariant factors must divide each other; elementary divisors are prime powers. Do not mix the two lists.
7. Jordan form needs the characteristic polynomial to split; rational canonical form does not.
