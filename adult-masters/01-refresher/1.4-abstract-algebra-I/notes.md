# 1.4 Abstract Algebra I: Study Notes

These notes condense a first course in groups and rings. Proofs are given in full where they are short and central, and sketched where the idea matters more than the details. Notation: $e$ or $1$ is the identity of a group $G$; $|G|$ is its order; $|g|$ is the order of the element $g$; $H\le G$ means $H$ is a subgroup; $N\trianglelefteq G$ means $N$ is a normal subgroup.

---

# Part I: Groups

## 1. Groups and subgroups

A *group* is a set $G$ with an associative binary operation, an identity element and inverses. It is *abelian* if the operation is commutative.

**Standard examples**
- $(\mathbb{Z},+)$ and $(\mathbb{Z}_n,+)$, which are cyclic.
- $U(n)=\lbrace [a]\in\mathbb{Z}_n:\gcd(a,n)=1\rbrace$ under multiplication, of order $\varphi(n)$.
- $D_n$, the symmetries of a regular $n$-gon, of order $2n$: $D_n=\langle r,s\mid r^n=s^2=e,\ srs^{-1}=r^{-1}\rangle$.
- $S_n$, all permutations of $\lbrace 1,\dots,n\rbrace$, of order $n!$.
- $GL_n(\mathbb{F})$ (invertible matrices), $SL_n(\mathbb{F})$ (determinant 1), $O(n)$ and $U(n)$.
- $Q_8=\lbrace\pm1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k\rbrace$ with $i^2=j^2=k^2=ijk=-1$.

**Subgroup test.** A nonempty subset $H\subseteq G$ is a subgroup iff $a,b\in H\Rightarrow ab^{-1}\in H$. For finite $H$, closure under the operation is enough.

**Order.** $|g|$ is the least $n\ge1$ with $g^n=e$, or $\infty$ if there is none. If $|g|=n$, then $g^k=e\iff n\mid k$, and $|g^k|=n/\gcd(n,k)$.

**Pitfalls.**
- $(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}$, not $a^{-1}b^{-1}$.
- $|ab|$ need not relate to $|a|$ and $|b|$ in a non-abelian group. In $S_3$, two elements of order 2 have a product of order 3.

## 2. Cyclic groups

$\langle g\rangle=\lbrace g^k:k\in\mathbb{Z}\rbrace$. A group is *cyclic* if $G=\langle g\rangle$ for some $g$. Every cyclic group is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$ or to some $\mathbb{Z}_n$.

**Theorem.** Every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic. If $|G|=n$ and $G$ is cyclic, then for each $d\mid n$ there is **exactly one** subgroup of order $d$, namely $\langle g^{n/d}\rangle$. The generators of $\mathbb{Z}_n$ are the $k$ with $\gcd(k,n)=1$.

*Proof that subgroups are cyclic.* Let $H\le\langle g\rangle$ with $H\neq\lbrace e\rbrace$. Let $m$ be the least positive integer with $g^m\in H$. If $g^k\in H$, write $k=qm+r$ with $0\le r<m$. Then $g^r=g^k(g^m)^{-q}\in H$, so $r=0$ by minimality. Hence $H=\langle g^m\rangle$. $\square$

## 3. Permutation groups

Every permutation is a product of disjoint cycles, uniquely up to order. Its *cycle type* is the multiset of cycle lengths. The order of a permutation is the lcm of its cycle lengths.

Every permutation is also a product of transpositions. The parity of the number of transpositions is well defined, which gives the **sign** homomorphism $\mathrm{sgn}:S_n\to\lbrace\pm1\rbrace$. A $k$-cycle has sign $(-1)^{k-1}$. The kernel of $\mathrm{sgn}$ is the alternating group $A_n$, of order $n!/2$ for $n\ge2$.

**Conjugation in $S_n$.**

$$
\sigma(a_1\,a_2\,\dots\,a_k)\sigma^{-1}=(\sigma(a_1)\,\sigma(a_2)\,\dots\,\sigma(a_k)).
$$

So two permutations are conjugate in $S_n$ iff they have the same cycle type. Conjugacy classes of $S_n$ correspond to partitions of $n$. In $A_n$ a class of $S_n$ can split into two; see the $A_5$ computation in Section 9.

## 4. Cosets and Lagrange's theorem

For $H\le G$, the *left cosets* $gH$ partition $G$, because $aH=bH\iff a^{-1}b\in H$. Each coset has $|H|$ elements, since $h\mapsto gh$ is a bijection $H\to gH$.

**Lagrange's Theorem.** If $G$ is finite and $H\le G$, then $|H|$ divides $|G|$, and $[G:H]=|G|/|H|$ is the number of cosets.

**Corollaries.**
- $|g|$ divides $|G|$, so $g^{|G|}=e$.
- A group of prime order is cyclic, generated by any non-identity element.
- **Euler:** $a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv1\pmod n$ if $\gcd(a,n)=1$. **Fermat:** $a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p$ if $p\nmid a$.

**The converse of Lagrange is false.** $A_4$ has order 12 but no subgroup of order 6 (Problem 14). Partial converses are given by Cauchy's theorem (Section 8) and Sylow's theorems (Section 9).

**Product formula.** For subgroups $H,K$, $|HK|=|H||K|/|H\cap K|$. Here $HK$ need not be a subgroup. It is one iff $HK=KH$, which holds for example when one of $H,K$ is normal.

## 5. Homomorphisms, normal subgroups and quotients

A *homomorphism* is a map $\varphi:G\to H$ with $\varphi(ab)=\varphi(a)\varphi(b)$. It follows that $\varphi(e)=e$ and $\varphi(a^{-1})=\varphi(a)^{-1}$. The *kernel* $\ker\varphi$ is a subgroup, and $\varphi$ is injective iff $\ker\varphi=\lbrace e\rbrace$.

**Normal subgroups.** $N\le G$ is *normal* if $gNg^{-1}=N$ for all $g$. Equivalent conditions:
- $gNg^{-1}\subseteq N$ for all $g$;
- $gN=Ng$ for all $g$;
- $N$ is a union of conjugacy classes;
- $N$ is the kernel of some homomorphism.

Kernels are normal: if $\varphi(n)=e$, then $\varphi(gng^{-1})=\varphi(g)\varphi(g)^{-1}=e$. Every subgroup of an abelian group is normal. Every subgroup of index 2 is normal (Problem 9).

**Quotient group.** If $N\trianglelefteq G$, the cosets form a group $G/N$ with $(aN)(bN)=abN$. Normality is **exactly** what makes this well defined. If $aN=a'N$ and $bN=b'N$, write $a'=an_1$ and $b'=bn_2$. Then $a'b'=an_1bn_2=ab(b^{-1}n_1b)n_2\in abN$. The map $\pi:G\to G/N$ is a surjective homomorphism with kernel $N$.

**First Isomorphism Theorem.** If $\varphi:G\to H$ is a homomorphism, then $G/\ker\varphi\cong\mathrm{im}\,\varphi$, via $g\ker\varphi\mapsto\varphi(g)$.

*Proof.* The map is well defined and injective because $\varphi(a)=\varphi(b)\iff a^{-1}b\in\ker\varphi\iff a\ker\varphi=b\ker\varphi$. It is a homomorphism because $\varphi$ is, and it is clearly onto $\mathrm{im}\,\varphi$. $\square$

**How to use it.** To identify $G/N$, find a surjective homomorphism out of $G$ whose kernel is $N$. Examples:
- $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}_n$.
- $GL_n/SL_n\cong\mathbb{F}^\times$, via $\det$.
- $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\cong S^1$, via $t\mapsto e^{2\pi it}$.
- $S_n/A_n\cong\lbrace\pm1\rbrace$.

**Second (diamond) Isomorphism Theorem.** If $H\le G$ and $N\trianglelefteq G$, then $HN\le G$, $H\cap N\trianglelefteq H$, and $HN/N\cong H/(H\cap N)$.

**Third Isomorphism Theorem.** If $N\le K$ are both normal in $G$, then $(G/N)/(K/N)\cong G/K$.

**Correspondence Theorem.** For $N\trianglelefteq G$, subgroups of $G/N$ correspond bijectively to subgroups of $G$ containing $N$, via $K\mapsto K/N$. The correspondence preserves inclusion, index and normality.

**Simple groups.** $G\neq\lbrace e\rbrace$ is *simple* if its only normal subgroups are $\lbrace e\rbrace$ and $G$. The abelian simple groups are exactly the $\mathbb{Z}_p$. Simple groups are the "atoms" from which all finite groups are built (Jordan–Hölder).

## 6. Direct products and finite abelian groups

$G\times H$ has the componentwise operation, and $|(g,h)|=\mathrm{lcm}(|g|,|h|)$. Hence $\mathbb{Z}_m\times\mathbb{Z}_n\cong\mathbb{Z}_{mn}$ iff $\gcd(m,n)=1$.

**Internal direct product.** If $H,K\trianglelefteq G$, $H\cap K=\lbrace e\rbrace$ and $HK=G$, then $G\cong H\times K$. The key step: for $h\in H$ and $k\in K$, the commutator $hkh^{-1}k^{-1}$ lies in $H\cap K$, so $hk=kh$.

**Fundamental Theorem of Finite Abelian Groups (statement).** Every finite abelian group is isomorphic to a product $\mathbb{Z}_{p_1^{a_1}}\times\dots\times\mathbb{Z}_{p_r^{a_r}}$ of cyclic groups of prime-power order (the primes need not be distinct), uniquely up to order. Equivalently, it is $\mathbb{Z}_{d_1}\times\dots\times\mathbb{Z}_{d_s}$ with $d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_s$ (invariant factors).

*Example.* The abelian groups of order $72=2^3\cdot3^2$ correspond to a partition of 3 and a partition of 2, so there are $3\times2=6$ of them.

This theorem is a special case of the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a PID, which is proved in 2.3/3.3. The same theorem gives the Jordan form from [1.3](../1.3-linear-algebra/notes.md).

## 7. Group actions

An *action* of $G$ on a set $X$ is a map $G\times X\to X$, $(g,x)\mapsto g\cdot x$, with $e\cdot x=x$ and $g\cdot(h\cdot x)=(gh)\cdot x$. Equivalently, it is a homomorphism $G\to\mathrm{Sym}(X)$.
- The *orbit* of $x$ is $\mathcal{O}_x=G\cdot x$. The orbits partition $X$.
- The *stabiliser* of $x$ is $G_x=\lbrace g:g\cdot x=x\rbrace\le G$.

**Orbit–Stabiliser Theorem.** The map $gG_x\mapsto g\cdot x$ is a well-defined bijection from $G/G_x$ (the set of cosets) to $\mathcal{O}_x$. So if $G$ is finite, $|\mathcal{O}_x|=[G:G_x]$ and

$$
|G|=|\mathcal{O}_x|\cdot|G_x|.
$$

*Example.* The rotation group of a cube acts transitively on the 6 faces. The stabiliser of a face is the 4 rotations about the axis through that face's centre. So the group has order 24. It is isomorphic to $S_4$, acting on the 4 long diagonals.

**Key actions.**
1. **Left multiplication** on $G$ itself. This gives **Cayley's Theorem**: every group embeds in $\mathrm{Sym}(G)$, so every group of order $n$ embeds in $S_n$.
2. **Left multiplication on cosets** $G/H$. This gives a homomorphism $G\to S_{[G:H]}$ whose kernel lies in $H$. *Useful consequence:* if $G$ is simple and $[G:H]=k>1$, then $G$ embeds in $S_k$, so $|G|$ divides $k!$.
3. **Conjugation** $g\cdot x=gxg^{-1}$. The orbits are conjugacy classes. The stabiliser of $x$ is the centraliser $C_G(x)$. The fixed points are the centre $Z(G)$.

**Class Equation.** If $G$ is finite and $x_1,\dots,x_r$ are representatives of the conjugacy classes with more than one element, then

$$
|G|=|Z(G)|+\sum_{i=1}^r[G:C_G(x_i)].
$$

**Corollary.** A nontrivial finite $p$-group (order $p^n$, $n\ge1$) has nontrivial centre. *Proof.* Each $[G:C_G(x_i)]$ is a power of $p$ greater than 1, so it is divisible by $p$. Hence $p$ divides $|Z(G)|$, and $|Z(G)|\ge p$. $\square$ Consequence: every group of order $p^2$ is abelian (Problem 13).

**Burnside's counting lemma.** The number of orbits equals $\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}|\mathrm{Fix}(g)|$. It is the standard tool for counting colourings up to symmetry.

## 8. Cauchy's theorem

**Cauchy.** If a prime $p$ divides $|G|$, then $G$ has an element of order $p$.

*Slick proof (McKay).* Let $X=\lbrace(g_1,\dots,g_p)\in G^p: g_1g_2\cdots g_p=e\rbrace$. Then $|X|=|G|^{p-1}$, because the first $p-1$ entries are free and determine the last. $\mathbb{Z}_p$ acts on $X$ by cyclic shifts; a shift of a solution is still a solution, since $ab=e \Rightarrow ba=e$. Orbits have size 1 or $p$. Since $p\mid|X|$, the number of fixed points is divisible by $p$. The fixed points are the tuples $(g,\dots,g)$ with $g^p=e$. One of them is $(e,\dots,e)$, so there are at least $p-1\ge1$ others, and each gives an element of order $p$. $\square$

## 9. Sylow theorems

Write $|G|=p^am$ with $p\nmid m$. A *Sylow $p$-subgroup* is a subgroup of order $p^a$. Let $n_p$ be the number of Sylow $p$-subgroups.

**Sylow I (existence).** Sylow $p$-subgroups exist. More generally, $G$ has a subgroup of every order $p^k$ with $k\le a$.
**Sylow II (conjugacy).** Any two Sylow $p$-subgroups are conjugate. Every $p$-subgroup is contained in some Sylow $p$-subgroup.
**Sylow III (counting).** $n_p\equiv1\pmod p$, $n_p\mid m$, and $n_p=[G:N_G(P)]$ for any Sylow $p$-subgroup $P$.

*Proof ideas.*
- I: either Wielandt's argument (let $G$ act on the set of all $p^a$-element subsets and count mod $p$), or induction on $|G|$ using the class equation and Cauchy.
- II: let a $p$-subgroup $Q$ act on the cosets $G/P$. The number of cosets $[G:P]=m$ is prime to $p$, so the action has a fixed point $gP$. That means $Q\subseteq gPg^{-1}$.
- III: $n_p\mid m$ because $n_p = [G:N_G(P)]$ by orbit–stabiliser for the conjugation action, and $P \le N_G(P)$. For $n_p\equiv1 \pmod p$, let $P$ act on the set of Sylow subgroups by conjugation. $P$ is the only fixed point, and all other orbits have size divisible by $p$.

**Key consequence.** $n_p=1$ iff the Sylow $p$-subgroup is normal.

**Standard applications.**
- *$|G|=pq$ with $p<q$ primes and $p\nmid q-1$* ⇒ $G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{pq}$. Examples: 15, 33, 35. *Proof:* $n_q\mid p$ and $n_q\equiv1\pmod q$ force $n_q=1$. $n_p\mid q$ and $n_p\equiv1\pmod p$ force $n_p=1$, since $q\not\equiv1\pmod p$. So both Sylow subgroups are normal, they intersect trivially, and $G\cong\mathbb{Z}_p\times\mathbb{Z}_q\cong\mathbb{Z}_{pq}$.
- *No simple group of order $n$.* Find a prime $p$ with $n_p$ forced to be 1. If that fails, count elements: distinct subgroups of prime order $p$ intersect trivially, so $n_p$ of them contribute $n_p(p-1)$ elements of order $p$. If these counts exceed $n$, some $n_p = 1$. Orders 12, 20, 30, 56 and 63 are all handled this way.
- **$A_5$ is simple.** Its class sizes are $1, 15, 20, 12, 12$. A normal subgroup is a union of classes containing $\lbrace e\rbrace$ whose size divides 60, and only $1$ and $60$ qualify. More generally, $A_n$ is simple for $n\ge5$. $A_5$ (order 60) is the smallest non-abelian simple group.

# Part II: Rings

## 10. Rings, ideals and quotients

A *ring* $R$ has addition (an abelian group) and an associative multiplication that distributes over addition. In this course **rings have a $1$** and are usually commutative. A *unit* is an element with a multiplicative inverse; the units form a group $R^\times$. A *zero divisor* is $a\neq0$ with $ab=0$ for some $b\neq0$.
- An *integral domain* is a commutative ring with $1\neq0$ and no zero divisors.
- A *field* is a commutative ring with $1\neq0$ in which every nonzero element is a unit.
- Every finite integral domain is a field: $x\mapsto ax$ is injective, hence surjective.

**Examples.** $\mathbb{Z}$; $\mathbb{Z}_n$ (a field iff $n$ is prime); $\mathbb{Q},\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}$; $R[x]$; $M_n(R)$ (non-commutative); $\mathbb{Z}[i]$; $C[0,1]$ (has zero divisors); $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$.

**Ideals.** An *ideal* $I\subseteq R$ is an additive subgroup with $rI\subseteq I$ for all $r\in R$. Ideals are exactly the kernels of ring homomorphisms. If an ideal contains a unit, it is all of $R$. Notation: $(a)=aR$ is a principal ideal, and $(a_1,\dots,a_n)$ is the ideal generated by $a_1,\dots,a_n$.

**Quotient ring.** $R/I$ consists of the additive cosets $a+I$, with $(a+I)(b+I)=ab+I$. This is well defined *because* $I$ absorbs multiplication. The isomorphism theorems and the correspondence theorem hold just as for groups. In particular $R/\ker\varphi\cong\mathrm{im}\,\varphi$.

**Characteristic.** $\mathrm{char}\,R$ is the least $n\ge1$ with $n\cdot1=0$, or $0$ if there is none. The characteristic of an integral domain is $0$ or a prime.

## 11. Prime and maximal ideals

Let $R$ be commutative. An ideal $P\neq R$ is *prime* if $ab\in P\Rightarrow a\in P$ or $b\in P$. An ideal $M\neq R$ is *maximal* if there is no ideal strictly between $M$ and $R$.

**Theorem.**
- $R/P$ is an integral domain iff $P$ is prime.
- $R/M$ is a field iff $M$ is maximal.
- Hence every maximal ideal is prime.

*Proof of the field statement.* By the correspondence theorem, the ideals of $R/M$ correspond to ideals of $R$ containing $M$. A commutative ring with $1\neq0$ is a field iff its only ideals are $0$ and itself. $\square$

**Examples in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$.**
- $(x)$ is prime but not maximal: $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x)\cong\mathbb{Z}$.
- $(2,x)$ is maximal: $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(2,x)\cong\mathbb{F}_2$.
- $(x^2+1)$ is prime, since $\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^2+1)\cong\mathbb{Z}[i]$.

**Field of fractions.** Every integral domain $D$ embeds in a smallest field $\mathrm{Frac}(D)$, consisting of formal fractions $a/b$ with $a/b=c/d\iff ad=bc$. For example, $\mathrm{Frac}(\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Q}$ and $\mathrm{Frac}(F[x])=F(x)$.

**Chinese Remainder Theorem.** If $I+J=R$ (the ideals are *comaximal*), then $R/(I\cap J)\cong R/I\times R/J$, and $I\cap J=IJ$ when $R$ is commutative. For $R=\mathbb{Z}$: $\mathbb{Z}_{mn}\cong\mathbb{Z}_m\times\mathbb{Z}_n$ as rings when $\gcd(m,n)=1$. Taking units gives $U(mn)\cong U(m)\times U(n)$, so $\varphi$ is multiplicative.

## 12. Factorisation: Euclidean domains, PIDs and UFDs

In an integral domain $D$:
- $a$ and $b$ are *associates* if $a=ub$ for a unit $u$.
- A non-unit $p\neq0$ is *irreducible* if $p=ab$ implies $a$ or $b$ is a unit.
- A non-unit $p\neq0$ is *prime* if $p\mid ab$ implies $p\mid a$ or $p\mid b$.
- Prime ⇒ irreducible always. The converse holds in UFDs but can fail in general.

**Definitions.**
- *Euclidean domain:* there is a norm $N:D\setminus\lbrace0\rbrace\to\mathbb{Z}_{\ge0}$ with a division algorithm. For all $a$ and all $b\neq0$, there are $q,r$ with $a=qb+r$ and either $r=0$ or $N(r)<N(b)$.
- *PID:* an integral domain in which every ideal is principal.
- *UFD:* every nonzero non-unit is a product of irreducibles, uniquely up to order and associates.

**Hierarchy.**

$$
\text{fields}\subset\text{Euclidean domains}\subset\text{PIDs}\subset\text{UFDs}\subset\text{integral domains}.
$$

- **ED ⇒ PID.** Given an ideal $I\neq0$, pick $b\in I$ of minimal norm. For $a\in I$, divide: $a = qb + r$. Then $r=a-qb\in I$ has smaller norm than $b$, so $r=0$. Hence $I=(b)$.
- **PID ⇒ UFD.** Existence: in a PID, ascending chains of ideals stabilise (the union of a chain is an ideal, hence principal, and its generator lies in some member of the chain). Uniqueness: in a PID, irreducibles are prime. If $p$ is irreducible, $(p)$ is maximal among principal ideals, hence maximal, hence prime.
- **In a PID, every nonzero prime ideal is maximal** (Problem 22).

**Examples and counterexamples.**

| Ring | ED? | PID? | UFD? |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathbb{Z}$, $F[x]$, $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ | yes | yes | yes |
| $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1+\sqrt{-19}}2]$ | no | yes | yes |
| $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, $F[x,y]$ | no | no | yes |
| $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$ | no | no | no |

- $\mathbb{Z}[i]$ is Euclidean with $N(a+bi)=a^2+b^2$. To divide, round the complex quotient to the nearest Gaussian integer; the error has norm at most $1/2$.
- $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is a UFD by Gauss's lemma, but $(2,x)$ is not principal.
- In $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, $6=2\cdot3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ gives two essentially different factorisations into irreducibles.
- The $\mathbb{Z}[\tfrac{1+\sqrt{-19}}2]$ example is standard but hard to verify (D&F 8.2); treat it as a known fact.

**Fact.** If $R$ is a UFD, then $R[x]$ is a UFD. Hence $\mathbb{Z}[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ and $F[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ are UFDs.

**Pitfall.** Gcds exist in any UFD, but **Bézout** ($\gcd(a,b)=ra+sb$) needs a PID. In $\mathbb{Z}[x]$, $\gcd(2,x)=1$, yet $1\notin(2,x)$.

## 13. Polynomial rings

**Division algorithm.** Let $F$ be a field, $f,g\in F[x]$ and $g\neq0$. There are unique $q,r$ with $f=qg+r$ and $\deg r<\deg g$. So $F[x]$ is a Euclidean domain (norm = degree), hence a PID and a UFD.

**Roots.** $a$ is a root of $f$ iff $(x-a)\mid f$. A nonzero polynomial of degree $n$ over an integral domain has at most $n$ roots. *Consequence:* any finite subgroup of $F^\times$ is cyclic. *Sketch:* if it were not cyclic, its exponent $m$ would be smaller than its order, but then $x^m-1$ would have more than $m$ roots. In particular, $\mathbb{F}_q^\times$ is cyclic.

**Irreducibility tests over $\mathbb{Q}$.**
1. *Degree 2 or 3:* irreducible iff there is no root in $F$. This fails for degree 4: $(x^2+1)^2$ has no rational root but is reducible.
2. *Rational root test:* a root $r/s$ in lowest terms of $a_nx^n+\dots+a_0\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$ has $r\mid a_0$ and $s\mid a_n$.
3. **Gauss's lemma:** a primitive $f\in\mathbb{Z}[x]$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$ iff it is irreducible in $\mathbb{Q}[x]$. Equivalently, if $f$ factors over $\mathbb{Q}$, it factors over $\mathbb{Z}$ with factors of the same degrees.
4. **Eisenstein:** if a prime $p$ divides every coefficient except the leading one, $p\nmid a_n$, and $p^2\nmid a_0$, then $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. A substitution $x\mapsto x+1$ often helps: $\Phi_p(x)=\frac{x^p-1}{x-1}$ becomes Eisenstein at $p$.
5. *Reduction mod $p$:* if $p\nmid a_n$ and $\bar f$ is irreducible in $\mathbb{F}_p[x]$, then $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. The converse fails: $x^4+1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ but reducible mod every prime.

**Constructing fields.** If $f\in F[x]$ is irreducible of degree $n$, then $(f)$ is maximal, and $K=F[x]/(f)$ is a field. It is an $n$-dimensional vector space over $F$ with basis $1,\alpha,\dots,\alpha^{n-1}$, where $\alpha=x+(f)$ is a root of $f$. This is **Kronecker's construction**, and it is how every finite field arises: $\mathbb{F}_{p^n}=\mathbb{F}_p[x]/(f)$ for any irreducible $f$ of degree $n$.

*Examples.*
- $\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1)\cong\mathbb{C}$.
- $\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^3-2)\cong\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$.
- $\mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^2+x+1)=\mathbb{F}_4$.

## Common pitfalls (summary)

1. Assuming the converse of Lagrange. $A_4$ has no subgroup of order 6.
2. Defining a quotient $G/H$ when $H$ is not normal. The coset multiplication is then not well defined.
3. Forgetting to check that a map out of a quotient is *well defined*.
4. Concluding $G$ is not simple because $n_p>1$. You need some $n_p=1$, or another argument such as counting elements or embedding in $S_k$.
5. Mixing up irreducible and prime elements. They coincide in UFDs, not in general.
6. Applying Eisenstein to a non-primitive polynomial, or forgetting the $p^2\nmid a_0$ condition.
7. "No roots ⇒ irreducible" only holds in degree 2 or 3.

## Connections forward

- **2.3 Abstract Algebra II:** field extensions, splitting fields, Galois groups. The Galois correspondence is a correspondence theorem between subgroups and intermediate fields. Solvability of groups decides solvability by radicals. Modules over PIDs give both the structure theorem for abelian groups and the Jordan form.
- **3.3 Graduate Algebra:** Noetherian rings, localisation, tensor products, representation theory of finite groups (built on conjugacy classes and group actions), and homological algebra.
- **3.4 Algebraic Topology:** the fundamental group. Covering spaces correspond to subgroups of $\pi_1$ (another Galois-type correspondence), and deck transformations are a group action. Homology groups are abelian groups computed as quotients, and the structure theorem describes them.
- **3.5 Smooth Manifolds:** Lie groups such as $GL_n$, $O(n)$ and $SU(2)$ act smoothly on manifolds, and quotients by group actions give new manifolds.
- **3.1 / 3.2 Analysis:** quotient constructions ($L^p$, quotient Banach spaces) follow the same "kernel of a homomorphism" pattern. In Banach algebras, maximal ideals correspond to characters (Gelfand theory).
