## 1. Homotopy and CW complexes

### 1.1 Homotopy

A **homotopy** between maps $f_0,f_1:X\to Y$ is a map $F:X\times I\to Y$ with $F(\cdot,0)=f_0$ and $F(\cdot,1)=f_1$. Write $f_0\simeq f_1$. If $F(a,t)$ is independent of $t$ for $a\in A\subseteq X$, the homotopy is **rel $A$**.

A map $f:X\to Y$ is a **homotopy equivalence** if there is $g:Y\to X$ with $gf\simeq \mathrm{id}_X$ and $fg\simeq\mathrm{id}_Y$. $X$ is **contractible** if it is homotopy equivalent to a point.

A **retraction** $r:X\to A$ is a map with $r|_A=\mathrm{id}_A$. A **deformation retraction** is a homotopy rel $A$ from $\mathrm{id}_X$ to a retraction onto $A$. If $A$ is a deformation retract of $X$, the inclusion $A\hookrightarrow X$ is a homotopy equivalence.

**Examples.** $\mathbb{R}^n\setminus\lbrace 0\rbrace$ deformation retracts onto $S^{n-1}$ via $F(x,t)=(1-t)x+t\,x/|x|$. The Möbius band deformation retracts onto its core circle. A torus minus a point deformation retracts onto $S^1\vee S^1$ (push out radially from the missing point in the square model).

**Key idea.** Every invariant in this course ($\pi_1$, $H_n$, $H^n$) is a functor that is *homotopy invariant*: homotopic maps induce the same homomorphism, so homotopy equivalent spaces have isomorphic invariants.

### 1.2 CW complexes

A **CW complex** is built inductively: $X^0$ is a discrete set; $X^n$ is obtained from $X^{n-1}$ by attaching $n$-cells $e^n_\alpha$ via maps $\varphi_\alpha:S^{n-1}\to X^{n-1}$, i.e. $X^n=\big(X^{n-1}\sqcup_\alpha D^n_\alpha\big)/(x\sim\varphi_\alpha(x))$; $X=\bigcup_n X^n$ with the weak topology (a set is closed iff it meets each closed cell in a closed set).

Standard examples:

| Space | Cells |
|---|---|
| $S^n$ | one 0-cell, one $n$-cell (attached by the constant map) |
| $\mathbb{RP}^n$ | one cell in each dimension $0,\dots,n$; $e^k$ attached by the 2-to-1 quotient $S^{k-1}\to\mathbb{RP}^{k-1}$ |
| $\mathbb{CP}^n$ | one cell in each even dimension $0,2,\dots,2n$ |
| closed orientable surface $M_g$ | one 0-cell, $2g$ 1-cells $a_i,b_i$, one 2-cell attached along $[a_1,b_1]\cdots[a_g,b_g]$ |
| closed non-orientable $N_h$ | one 0-cell, $h$ 1-cells, one 2-cell attached along $a_1^2\cdots a_h^2$ |

**Homotopy extension property (HEP).** A CW pair $(X,A)$ (with $A$ a subcomplex) has the HEP: any homotopy of $A$ extends to $X$ given an extension at time 0. Consequence: if $A$ is a contractible subcomplex, then $X\to X/A$ is a homotopy equivalence. For example, collapsing a maximal tree in a connected graph shows every connected graph is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of circles.

**Operations.** Wedge sum $X\vee Y$, suspension $SX$, cone $CX$, smash product. $SS^n\cong S^{n+1}$.

## 2. The fundamental group

### 2.1 Definition

For $x_0\in X$, $\pi_1(X,x_0)$ is the set of homotopy classes rel endpoints of loops $\gamma:I\to X$ with $\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)=x_0$, under concatenation $[\gamma][\delta]=[\gamma\cdot\delta]$. It is a group (identity: constant loop; inverse: reversed loop). A path $h$ from $x_0$ to $x_1$ gives an isomorphism $\beta_h:\pi_1(X,x_1)\to\pi_1(X,x_0)$, $[\gamma]\mapsto[h\cdot\gamma\cdot\bar h]$, so for path-connected $X$ the group is well defined up to isomorphism. $X$ is **simply connected** if path-connected with trivial $\pi_1$.

A map $f:(X,x_0)\to(Y,y_0)$ induces $f_*:\pi_1(X,x_0)\to\pi_1(Y,y_0)$; $(gf)_*=g_*f_*$; $\mathrm{id}_*=\mathrm{id}$. A homotopy equivalence induces an isomorphism (with care about basepoints: if $f_t$ moves the basepoint along $h$, then $f_{0*}=\beta_h f_{1*}$).

### 2.2 $\pi_1(S^1)\cong\mathbb{Z}$

**Theorem.** $\pi_1(S^1,1)\cong\mathbb{Z}$, generated by the loop $\omega(s)=e^{2\pi i s}$.

*Proof sketch.* Let $p:\mathbb{R}\to S^1$, $p(s)=e^{2\pi i s}$. Two lifting facts:

1. **Path lifting.** For each path $\gamma$ in $S^1$ starting at $p(\tilde x_0)$ there is a unique lift $\tilde\gamma$ in $\mathbb{R}$ starting at $\tilde x_0$.
2. **Homotopy lifting.** A homotopy of paths $\gamma_t$ lifts uniquely to a homotopy $\tilde\gamma_t$ of lifts with fixed starting point.

Both are proved by a Lebesgue number argument: cover $S^1$ by evenly covered open arcs, subdivide $I$ (or $I\times I$) so each small piece maps into one arc, and lift piece by piece using the local homeomorphisms.

Define $\Phi:\pi_1(S^1,1)\to\mathbb{Z}$ by $\Phi[\gamma]=\tilde\gamma(1)$ where $\tilde\gamma$ is the lift starting at $0$. Homotopy lifting makes $\Phi$ well defined (endpoint is fixed along a homotopy rel endpoints, since it varies continuously in the discrete set $\mathbb{Z}$). It is a homomorphism since the lift of $\gamma\cdot\delta$ is $\tilde\gamma$ followed by $\tilde\delta+\tilde\gamma(1)$. Surjective: $\omega^n$ lifts to $s\mapsto ns$. Injective: if $\tilde\gamma(1)=0$, $\tilde\gamma$ is a loop in the contractible $\mathbb{R}$, so $\tilde\gamma\simeq$ const rel endpoints, and composing with $p$ gives $\gamma\simeq$ const. $\square$

**Applications.**
- **No retraction** $D^2\to S^1$: if $r$ were one, $r\circ i=\mathrm{id}_{S^1}$ gives $\mathbb{Z}\to 0\to\mathbb{Z}$ composing to the identity, impossible.
- **Brouwer (dim 2).** If $h:D^2\to D^2$ has no fixed point, the ray from $h(x)$ through $x$ hits $S^1$ at $r(x)$, a retraction. Contradiction.
- **Fundamental theorem of algebra.** If $p(z)=z^n+\dots$ has no root, the loops $f_r(s)=p(re^{2\pi is})/|p(re^{2\pi is})|$ are all homotopic (vary $r$), trivial at $r=0$, and homotopic to $\omega^n$ for large $r$; so $n=0$.
- **Borsuk–Ulam (dim 2).** Every map $S^2\to\mathbb{R}^2$ sends some pair of antipodal points to the same point.
- **Products.** $\pi_1(X\times Y)\cong\pi_1X\times\pi_1Y$; so $\pi_1(T^n)\cong\mathbb{Z}^n$.
- **Spheres.** $\pi_1(S^n)=0$ for $n\ge 2$ (a loop can be homotoped to miss a point, and $S^n\setminus\lbrace pt\rbrace\cong\mathbb{R}^n$). Pitfall: loops can be space-filling, so "homotope to miss a point" needs an argument (Hatcher uses Lebesgue numbers to make the loop piecewise linear near a point, or use van Kampen with two open hemispheres).

### 2.3 Seifert–van Kampen

The **free product** $G*H$ is the group of reduced words in $G\sqcup H$; it is the coproduct in the category of groups. An amalgamated product $G*_K H$ is $G*H$ modulo relations $i_1(k)=i_2(k)$.

**Theorem (van Kampen).** Let $X=\bigcup_\alpha A_\alpha$ with each $A_\alpha$ open, path-connected, containing $x_0$. If each $A_\alpha\cap A_\beta$ is path-connected, then $\Phi:*_\alpha\pi_1(A_\alpha)\to\pi_1(X)$ is surjective. If also each triple intersection is path-connected, $\ker\Phi$ is the normal subgroup $N$ generated by $i_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)i_{\beta\alpha}(\omega)^{-1}$ for $\omega\in\pi_1(A_\alpha\cap A_\beta)$. So
$$
\pi_1(X)\cong *_\alpha\pi_1(A_\alpha)\big/N.
$$
For two sets: $\pi_1(U\cup V)\cong\pi_1(U)*_{\pi_1(U\cap V)}\pi_1(V)$.

*Proof idea.* Surjectivity: subdivide a loop so each piece lies in one $A_\alpha$ and connect the subdivision points to $x_0$ by paths inside intersections. Injectivity modulo $N$: subdivide a nullhomotopy $I\times I$ into small rectangles, each mapping into one $A_\alpha$, and move across the grid one rectangle at a time; each move is either a relation inside one $\pi_1(A_\alpha)$ or a relation in $N$. The triple-intersection condition handles grid vertices touching three or more sets.

**Consequences.**
- $\pi_1(\bigvee_\alpha S^1)$ is free on the circles (need each wedge point to have a contractible neighborhood: true for CW complexes).
- **Attaching 2-cells.** If $Y$ is obtained from path-connected $X$ by attaching 2-cells along loops $\varphi_\alpha$, then $\pi_1(Y)\cong\pi_1(X)/N$, where $N$ is the normal closure of the $[\varphi_\alpha]$ (after connecting to the basepoint). Attaching $n$-cells for $n\ge3$ does not change $\pi_1$.
- Hence for any 2-dimensional CW complex with one 0-cell, $\pi_1$ has a presentation with one generator per 1-cell and one relator per 2-cell. **Every group is the fundamental group of some 2-dimensional CW complex.**
- Surfaces: $\pi_1(M_g)=\langle a_1,b_1,\dots,a_g,b_g\mid [a_1,b_1]\cdots[a_g,b_g]\rangle$; abelianization $\mathbb{Z}^{2g}$, so the $M_g$ are pairwise non-homotopy-equivalent.
- $\pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^2)=\mathbb{Z}/2$; Klein bottle $K$: $\langle a,b\mid abab^{-1}\rangle$.

**Pitfall.** The sets in van Kampen must be *open* (or deformation retract from open neighborhoods). Using closed sets like two closed hemispheres naively can fail for wild spaces.

## 3. Covering spaces

### 3.1 Definitions and lifting

A **covering space** of $X$ is a map $p:\tilde X\to X$ such that each $x\in X$ has an open neighborhood $U$ with $p^{-1}(U)$ a disjoint union of open sets each mapped homeomorphically onto $U$ ("evenly covered"). The sheets can be empty, so we usually require $\tilde X$ path-connected later.

**Homotopy lifting property.** Given a homotopy $f_t:Y\to X$ and a lift $\tilde f_0$ of $f_0$, there is a unique homotopy $\tilde f_t$ lifting $f_t$ that starts at $\tilde f_0$.

**Corollaries.**
- $p_*:\pi_1(\tilde X,\tilde x_0)\to\pi_1(X,x_0)$ is **injective**; its image consists of loops whose lifts at $\tilde x_0$ are loops.
- For path-connected $\tilde X$, $X$, the number of sheets equals the index $[\pi_1(X,x_0):p_*\pi_1(\tilde X,\tilde x_0)]$.

**Lifting criterion.** Let $Y$ be path-connected and locally path-connected, $f:(Y,y_0)\to(X,x_0)$. A lift $\tilde f:(Y,y_0)\to(\tilde X,\tilde x_0)$ exists iff $f_*\pi_1(Y,y_0)\subseteq p_*\pi_1(\tilde X,\tilde x_0)$.

*Proof sketch.* Define $\tilde f(y)$ as the endpoint of the lift of $f\circ\gamma$ for a path $\gamma$ from $y_0$ to $y$. The subgroup condition makes this independent of $\gamma$; local path-connectedness gives continuity.

**Counterexample showing local path-connectedness is needed.** The "quasi-circle" $Y$ (a closed topologist's sine curve with its ends joined by an arc) is simply connected, but the natural map $Y\to S^1$ does not lift to $\mathbb{R}$.

**Unique lifting.** Two lifts $Y\to\tilde X$ of the same map that agree at one point of a connected $Y$ agree everywhere.

### 3.2 Classification

$X$ is **semi-locally simply connected** if each point has a neighborhood $U$ such that $\pi_1(U,x)\to\pi_1(X,x)$ is trivial. CW complexes and manifolds qualify; the Hawaiian earring does not.

**Theorem (universal cover).** If $X$ is path-connected, locally path-connected and semi-locally simply connected, then $X$ has a simply connected covering space $\tilde X$. Construction: $\tilde X=\lbrace$homotopy classes rel endpoints of paths starting at $x_0\rbrace$, $p[\gamma]=\gamma(1)$, with a suitable topology.

**Theorem (classification; Galois correspondence).** For such $X$:
- Basepoint-preserving isomorphism classes of path-connected covers $(\tilde X,\tilde x_0)\to(X,x_0)$ correspond bijectively to subgroups $H\le\pi_1(X,x_0)$, via $\tilde X\mapsto p_*\pi_1(\tilde X,\tilde x_0)$.
- Ignoring basepoints, isomorphism classes of covers correspond to **conjugacy classes** of subgroups.

Build the cover for $H$ as $\tilde X_H=\tilde X/H$ (quotient of the universal cover by $H$ acting by deck transformations).

### 3.3 Deck transformations

A **deck transformation** is a homeomorphism $\tau:\tilde X\to\tilde X$ with $p\tau=p$. They form a group $G(\tilde X)$. A cover is **normal** (regular, Galois) if $G(\tilde X)$ acts transitively on each fibre; equivalently $H=p_*\pi_1(\tilde X)$ is normal in $\pi_1(X)$.

**Theorem.** $G(\tilde X)\cong N(H)/H$, where $N(H)$ is the normalizer. In particular for a normal cover $G(\tilde X)\cong\pi_1(X)/H$, and for the universal cover $G(\tilde X)\cong\pi_1(X)$.

**Covering space actions.** An action of $G$ on $Y$ is a *covering space action* if each $y$ has a neighborhood $U$ with $gU\cap U=\emptyset$ for $g\ne e$. Then $Y\to Y/G$ is a normal cover with deck group $G$. If $Y$ is simply connected (and path-connected, locally path-connected), then $\pi_1(Y/G)\cong G$.

**Examples.**
- $\mathbb{Z}^n$ acting on $\mathbb{R}^n$ by translation: $\pi_1(T^n)=\mathbb{Z}^n$.
- $\mathbb{Z}/2$ acting on $S^n$ by $\pm1$: $\pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^n)=\mathbb{Z}/2$ for $n\ge2$.
- $\mathbb{Z}/m$ acting on $S^3\subset\mathbb{C}^2$ by $(z,w)\mapsto(\zeta z,\zeta^q w)$, $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/m}$, $\gcd(q,m)=1$: lens space $L(m,q)$ with $\pi_1\cong\mathbb{Z}/m$.
- Covers of $S^1\vee S^1$ are exactly graphs where each vertex has one incoming and one outgoing $a$-edge and $b$-edge. This gives the topological proof that **subgroups of free groups are free** and the **Schreier index formula**: a subgroup of index $k$ in $F_n$ is free of rank $k(n-1)+1$.

## 4. Homology

### 4.1 Chain complexes

A **chain complex** $(C_\bullet,\partial)$ is a sequence of abelian groups with $\partial_n:C_n\to C_{n-1}$ and $\partial\partial=0$. Its homology is $H_n=\ker\partial_n/\mathrm{im}\,\partial_{n+1}$. A **chain map** $f_\#$ commutes with $\partial$ and induces $f_*$ on homology. A **chain homotopy** is $P:C_n\to D_{n+1}$ with $\partial P+P\partial=f_\#-g_\#$; chain homotopic maps induce the same map on homology.

**Zig-zag lemma.** A short exact sequence of chain complexes $0\to A_\bullet\to B_\bullet\to C_\bullet\to0$ gives a long exact sequence
$$
\cdots\to H_n(A)\to H_n(B)\to H_n(C)\xrightarrow{\ \partial\ }H_{n-1}(A)\to\cdots
$$
The connecting map: lift a cycle $c$ to $b\in B_n$, then $\partial b$ comes from a unique cycle $a\in A_{n-1}$; set $\partial[c]=[a]$. Do this diagram chase yourself once.

**Five lemma.** In a commutative ladder of exact rows, if the four outer maps are isomorphisms, so is the middle one.

### 4.2 Simplicial and singular homology

For a $\Delta$-complex $X$, $\Delta_n(X)$ is free abelian on the $n$-simplices and
$$
\partial\sigma=\sum_{i=0}^n(-1)^i\sigma|_{[v_0,\dots,\hat v_i,\dots,v_n]}.
$$
**Singular homology** uses all maps $\sigma:\Delta^n\to X$ as generators of $C_n(X)$ with the same formula. It is huge but obviously functorial and topologically invariant. **Reduced homology** $\tilde H_n$ uses the augmentation $C_0\to\mathbb{Z}$; $\tilde H_0(X)\oplus\mathbb{Z}\cong H_0(X)$, and $\tilde H_n=H_n$ for $n>0$.

**Basic facts.**
- $H_0(X)$ is free abelian on the path components.
- $H_n(\text{point})=0$ for $n>0$.
- **Homotopy invariance:** $f\simeq g\Rightarrow f_*=g_*$. (Proof: the prism operator subdivides $\Delta^n\times I$ into $(n+1)$-simplices and gives a chain homotopy.)
- **Relative homology** $H_n(X,A)$ from $C_\bullet(X)/C_\bullet(A)$, with long exact sequence
$$
\cdots\to H_n(A)\to H_n(X)\to H_n(X,A)\to H_{n-1}(A)\to\cdots
$$
- **Excision.** If $\overline Z\subseteq\mathrm{int}\,A$, then $H_n(X\setminus Z,A\setminus Z)\to H_n(X,A)$ is an isomorphism. Equivalently, for $A,B$ with interiors covering $X$, $H_n(B,A\cap B)\cong H_n(X,A)$. Proof uses barycentric subdivision to replace chains by "small" chains; this is the hardest technical proof of the course. Learn the statement and what it buys you.
- **Good pairs.** If $A$ is a nonempty closed subspace that is a deformation retract of a neighborhood (true for CW pairs), then $H_n(X,A)\cong\tilde H_n(X/A)$.
- Simplicial and singular homology agree for $\Delta$-complexes (five lemma plus induction on skeleta).

**Hurewicz in degree 1.** For path-connected $X$, the map $\pi_1(X)\to H_1(X)$ sending a loop to its singular 1-cycle induces $\pi_1(X)^{ab}\cong H_1(X)$.

### 4.3 Spheres, degree, and classical applications

**Theorem.** $\tilde H_k(S^n)=\mathbb{Z}$ for $k=n$ and $0$ otherwise.

*Proof.* Use the good pair $(D^n,S^{n-1})$ with $D^n/S^{n-1}\cong S^n$. Since $\tilde H_*(D^n)=0$, the long exact sequence gives $\tilde H_k(S^n)\cong H_k(D^n,S^{n-1})\cong\tilde H_{k-1}(S^{n-1})$. Induct from $S^0$. $\square$

**Corollaries.**
- $\partial D^n=S^{n-1}$ is not a retract of $D^n$; **Brouwer's fixed point theorem** holds in every dimension.
- **Invariance of dimension.** $\mathbb{R}^m\cong\mathbb{R}^n$ implies $m=n$: local homology $H_k(\mathbb{R}^n,\mathbb{R}^n\setminus\lbrace x\rbrace)\cong\tilde H_{k-1}(S^{n-1})$ is a topological invariant.

**Degree.** A map $f:S^n\to S^n$ induces multiplication by an integer $\deg f$ on $H_n(S^n)\cong\mathbb{Z}$. Properties:
1. $\deg\mathrm{id}=1$; $\deg(fg)=\deg f\deg g$; homotopic maps have equal degree; a non-surjective map has degree 0.
2. A reflection in a hyperplane has degree $-1$.
3. The antipodal map $-\mathrm{id}$ on $S^n$ is a composite of $n+1$ reflections, so has degree $(-1)^{n+1}$.
4. If $f$ has no fixed points, $f\simeq-\mathrm{id}$ via $\dfrac{(1-t)f(x)-tx}{|(1-t)f(x)-tx|}$, so $\deg f=(-1)^{n+1}$.
5. If $f(x)\ne -x$ for all $x$, then $f\simeq\mathrm{id}$ and $\deg f=1$.

**Hairy ball theorem.** $S^n$ has a nowhere-zero continuous tangent vector field iff $n$ is odd. If $v$ is such a field (normalized), $f_t(x)=\cos(\pi t)x+\sin(\pi t)v(x)$ is a homotopy from $\mathrm{id}$ to $-\mathrm{id}$, forcing $(-1)^{n+1}=1$. For $n=2k-1$ odd, $v(x_1,\dots,x_{2k})=(-x_2,x_1,\dots,-x_{2k},x_{2k-1})$ works.

**Local degree.** If $f^{-1}(y)=\lbrace x_1,\dots,x_m\rbrace$ is finite, $\deg f=\sum_i\deg f|_{x_i}$, where the local degree is computed in $H_n(U_i,U_i\setminus x_i)$. For smooth maps this is the signed count of preimages of a regular value, matching the smooth degree in [3.5](../3.5-smooth-manifolds/notes.md).

### 4.4 Mayer–Vietoris

If $X=\mathrm{int}\,A\cup\mathrm{int}\,B$, the short exact sequence $0\to C_\bullet(A\cap B)\to C_\bullet(A)\oplus C_\bullet(B)\to C_\bullet(A+B)\to0$ (where $C(A+B)$ are sums of chains in $A$ or $B$, which compute $H_*(X)$ by the small-chains theorem) gives
$$
\cdots\to H_n(A\cap B)\xrightarrow{(i_*,\,-j_*)}H_n(A)\oplus H_n(B)\xrightarrow{k_*+l_*}H_n(X)\xrightarrow{\partial}H_{n-1}(A\cap B)\to\cdots
$$
Same with reduced homology when $A\cap B\ne\emptyset$. The boundary map takes a cycle $z=a+b$ to $[\partial a]=-[\partial b]$.

**Example: Klein bottle.** Write $K$ as two Möbius bands $A,B$ glued along their boundary circle; $A\cap B\simeq S^1$, and $A,B\simeq S^1$. The boundary circle of a Möbius band wraps twice around its core. In degree 1: $H_1(A\cap B)=\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}$, $1\mapsto(2,-2)$, which is injective, so $H_2(K)=0$. Then $H_1(K)\cong(\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z})/\langle(2,-2)\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2$ (basis $(1,-1),(0,1)$; the subgroup is $2\cdot(1,-1)$), using that $\tilde H_0(A\cap B)=0$.

### 4.5 Cellular homology and Euler characteristic

For a CW complex $X$:
- $H_k(X^n,X^{n-1})$ is free abelian on the $n$-cells for $k=n$ and $0$ otherwise.
- $H_k(X^n)=0$ for $k>n$, and $H_k(X^n)\cong H_k(X)$ for $k<n$.

The **cellular chain complex** is $C_n^{CW}=H_n(X^n,X^{n-1})$ with $d_n$ the composite $H_n(X^n,X^{n-1})\to H_{n-1}(X^{n-1})\to H_{n-1}(X^{n-1},X^{n-2})$. **Theorem:** $H_n^{CW}(X)\cong H_n(X)$.

**Cellular boundary formula.**
$$
d_n(e^n_\alpha)=\sum_\beta d_{\alpha\beta}\,e^{n-1}_\beta,
$$
where $d_{\alpha\beta}$ is the degree of $S^{n-1}\xrightarrow{\varphi_\alpha}X^{n-1}\to X^{n-1}/(X^{n-1}\setminus e_\beta^{n-1})\cong S^{n-1}$. For $n=1$, $d_1$ is the usual vertex difference. For $n=2$, $d_{\alpha\beta}$ is the exponent sum of the letter $\beta$ in the attaching word.

**Example: $\mathbb{RP}^n$.** One cell $e^k$ for $0\le k\le n$. The attaching map $S^{k-1}\to\mathbb{RP}^{k-1}$ followed by the collapse to $S^{k-1}$ is a map whose local degrees at the two preimages are $1$ and $\deg(-\mathrm{id}_{S^{k-1}})=(-1)^k$. So $d_k=1+(-1)^k$: zero for $k$ odd, $2$ for $k$ even. The cellular complex is
$$
0\to\mathbb{Z}\xrightarrow{d_n}\cdots\xrightarrow{2}\mathbb{Z}\xrightarrow{0}\mathbb{Z}\xrightarrow{2}\mathbb{Z}\xrightarrow{0}\mathbb{Z}\to0,
$$
giving $H_0=\mathbb{Z}$; $H_k=\mathbb{Z}/2$ for $k$ odd, $0<k<n$; $H_k=0$ for $k$ even, $0<k\le n$; and $H_n=\mathbb{Z}$ if $n$ odd.

**Example: $\mathbb{CP}^n$.** Cells only in even degrees, so all $d=0$ and $H_{2k}=\mathbb{Z}$ for $0\le k\le n$, zero otherwise.

**Euler characteristic.** For a finite CW complex, $\chi(X)=\sum_n(-1)^nc_n$ where $c_n$ is the number of $n$-cells. **Theorem:** $\chi(X)=\sum_n(-1)^n\mathrm{rank}\,H_n(X)$. (Rank is additive along exact sequences of finitely generated abelian groups; apply to $0\to Z_n\to C_n\to B_{n-1}\to0$ and $0\to B_n\to Z_n\to H_n\to0$.) So $\chi$ is a homotopy invariant. $\chi(M_g)=2-2g$, $\chi(N_h)=2-h$, $\chi(S^n)=1+(-1)^n$. For an $n$-sheeted cover $\tilde X\to X$ of a finite CW complex, $\chi(\tilde X)=n\,\chi(X)$ (lift the cells).

### 4.6 Homology with coefficients

Replace $C_n(X)$ by $C_n(X)\otimes G$. With $G=\mathbb{Z}/2$, orientation signs disappear: $H_k(\mathbb{RP}^n;\mathbb{Z}/2)=\mathbb{Z}/2$ for $0\le k\le n$. Field coefficients make every homology group a vector space, and the Euler characteristic can be computed with any field.

## 5. Cohomology and cup product (light)

### 5.1 Cohomology

Dualize: $C^n(X;G)=\mathrm{Hom}(C_n(X),G)$, $\delta\varphi=\varphi\circ\partial$, so $\delta:C^n\to C^{n+1}$. Then $H^n(X;G)=\ker\delta/\mathrm{im}\,\delta$. Cohomology is contravariant: $f:X\to Y$ gives $f^*:H^n(Y)\to H^n(X)$. It satisfies homotopy invariance, long exact sequences, excision and Mayer–Vietoris, all with arrows reversed.

**Universal coefficient theorem.** There is a split short exact sequence
$$
0\to\mathrm{Ext}(H_{n-1}(X),G)\to H^n(X;G)\to\mathrm{Hom}(H_n(X),G)\to0.
$$
For finitely generated groups, $\mathrm{Ext}(\mathbb{Z},G)=0$ and $\mathrm{Ext}(\mathbb{Z}/m,G)=G/mG$. Consequence with $G=\mathbb{Z}$: if $H_n=\mathbb{Z}^{b_n}\oplus T_n$ (torsion $T_n$), then $H^n(X;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{b_n}\oplus T_{n-1}$. Torsion moves up one degree. Example: $H^2(\mathbb{RP}^2;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}/2$ while $H_2(\mathbb{RP}^2)=0$.

### 5.2 Cup product

For a commutative ring $R$, $\varphi\in C^k(X;R)$ and $\psi\in C^\ell(X;R)$,
$$
(\varphi\smile\psi)(\sigma)=\varphi\big(\sigma|_{[v_0,\dots,v_k]}\big)\,\psi\big(\sigma|_{[v_k,\dots,v_{k+\ell}]}\big).
$$
Then $\delta(\varphi\smile\psi)=\delta\varphi\smile\psi+(-1)^k\varphi\smile\delta\psi$, so $\smile$ descends to $H^k\times H^\ell\to H^{k+\ell}$. This makes $H^*(X;R)$ a graded ring that is **graded commutative**: $a\smile b=(-1)^{k\ell}b\smile a$. Induced maps $f^*$ are ring homomorphisms, so the ring structure is a homotopy invariant that is finer than the groups.

**Rings to know (statements; Hatcher §3.2).**
- $H^*(\mathbb{CP}^n;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^{n+1})$, $|x|=2$.
- $H^*(\mathbb{RP}^n;\mathbb{Z}/2)\cong\mathbb{Z}/2[x]/(x^{n+1})$, $|x|=1$.
- $H^*(T^n;\mathbb{Z})\cong\Lambda_{\mathbb{Z}}[x_1,\dots,x_n]$, $|x_i|=1$ (exterior algebra).
- $H^*(M_g;\mathbb{Z})$: $H^1=\mathbb{Z}^{2g}$ with basis $\alpha_i,\beta_i$, $\alpha_i\smile\beta_i=-\beta_i\smile\alpha_i$ a generator of $H^2$, all other products of basis elements zero.
- Wedge sums: $\tilde H^*(X\vee Y)\cong\tilde H^*(X)\times\tilde H^*(Y)$ as rings, so products of classes from different summands vanish.

**Example.** $\mathbb{CP}^2$ and $S^2\vee S^4$ have the same homology ($\mathbb{Z}$ in degrees $0,2,4$) but in $\mathbb{CP}^2$ the square of the degree-2 generator generates $H^4$, while in $S^2\vee S^4$ it is zero. So they are not homotopy equivalent. The same argument shows the Hopf map $S^3\to S^2$ is not nullhomotopic, because $\mathbb{CP}^2$ is $S^2$ with a 4-cell attached by the Hopf map.

## 6. Common pitfalls

1. **Basepoints.** $\pi_1$ is only defined up to non-canonical isomorphism when you move the basepoint (canonical only when $\pi_1$ is abelian). Homotopies of maps can move the basepoint.
2. **Van Kampen hypotheses.** Open sets, path-connected intersections. Two overlapping arcs covering $S^1$ have disconnected intersection, so you cannot conclude $\pi_1(S^1)=0$.
3. **Covering classification needs local niceness.** Without semi-local simple connectivity there may be no universal cover (Hawaiian earring).
4. **Signs in boundary maps.** Always check $\partial\partial=0$ on an example. In cellular homology, compute degrees of attaching maps carefully, especially with orientation-reversing identifications ($\mathbb{RP}^n$, Klein bottle).
5. **Homology does not detect everything.** The Poincaré homology sphere has the homology of $S^3$ but nontrivial $\pi_1$. $\mathbb{CP}^2$ and $S^2\vee S^4$ have equal homology but are distinguished by cup products.
6. **Contravariance.** Cohomology reverses arrows; the connecting map in Mayer–Vietoris goes $H^n(A\cap B)\to H^{n+1}(X)$.
7. **Torsion and Hom.** $\mathrm{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}/2,\mathbb{Z})=0$: torsion in $H_n$ is invisible in $\mathrm{Hom}(H_n,\mathbb{Z})$ and reappears in $H^{n+1}$ via Ext.
8. **$H_1$ is the abelianization, not $\pi_1$.** $S^1\vee S^1$ and $T^2$ minus a point both have $H_1=\mathbb{Z}^2$; they are in fact homotopy equivalent, but $T^2$ itself is not homotopy equivalent to $S^1\vee S^1$ ($H_2$ differs).

## 7. Connections forward

- **Smooth manifolds ([3.5](../3.5-smooth-manifolds/)).** de Rham's theorem: $H^k_{dR}(M)\cong H^k(M;\mathbb{R})$. Smooth degree equals homological degree. Orientation can be defined via local homology $H_n(M,M\setminus x)$, and the orientation double cover is a covering space.
- **Poincaré duality** (Hatcher §3.3): for a closed orientable $n$-manifold, $H^k(M)\cong H_{n-k}(M)$. The natural next step after this module.
- **Higher homotopy groups and fibrations** (Hatcher Ch. 4): long exact sequence of a fibration, Hurewicz theorem, Whitehead's theorem.
- **Complex analysis ([2.2](../../02-bridge/2.2-complex-analysis/)).** Winding numbers are degrees of maps to $S^1$; Riemann surfaces of multivalued functions are covering spaces; monodromy is the action of $\pi_1$ on fibres.
- **Algebra.** Group cohomology is cohomology of $K(G,1)$; covering theory mirrors Galois theory; the étale fundamental group in algebraic geometry is a direct analogue ([algebraic geometry elective](../../04-electives/algebraic-geometry/)).
- **Lie groups ([elective](../../04-electives/lie-groups-representation-theory/)).** $\pi_1(SO(3))=\mathbb{Z}/2$ with universal cover $SU(2)\cong S^3$.
