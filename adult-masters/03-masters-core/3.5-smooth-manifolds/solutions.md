## Warm-up

### 1. Stereographic atlas

For $u\in\mathbb{R}^n$,
$$
\sigma_N^{-1}(u)=\left(\frac{2u}{|u|^2+1},\ \frac{|u|^2-1}{|u|^2+1}\right).
$$
*Check:* this point has norm $\frac{4|u|^2+(|u|^2-1)^2}{(|u|^2+1)^2}=1$, and with $x^{n+1}=\frac{|u|^2-1}{|u|^2+1}$ we get $1-x^{n+1}=\frac{2}{|u|^2+1}$, so $\sigma_N$ of it is $\frac{2u}{|u|^2+1}\cdot\frac{|u|^2+1}{2}=u$. Conversely $\sigma_N^{-1}\circ\sigma_N=\mathrm{id}$ by the same computation (or because $\sigma_N$ is injective: the line through $N$ and $x$ meets the sphere in only one other point). Both maps are continuous, so $\sigma_N$ is a homeomorphism onto $\mathbb{R}^n$; similarly for $\sigma_S$.

The overlap is $S^n\setminus\lbrace N,S\rbrace$, and $\sigma_N$ maps it onto $\mathbb{R}^n\setminus\lbrace0\rbrace$. For $u\ne0$, $1+x^{n+1}=\frac{2|u|^2}{|u|^2+1}$, so
$$
\sigma_S\circ\sigma_N^{-1}(u)=\frac{2u}{|u|^2+1}\cdot\frac{|u|^2+1}{2|u|^2}=\frac{u}{|u|^2}.
$$
This is smooth on $\mathbb{R}^n\setminus0$ and is its own inverse, so it is a diffeomorphism. The two charts cover $S^n$, so they form a smooth atlas. $\square$

### 2. Hyperboloids and the cone

(a) $df_{(x,y,z)}=2x\,dx+2y\,dy-2z\,dz$ vanishes only at the origin, and $f(0)=0$. So every $c\ne0$ is a regular value and $f^{-1}(c)$ is an embedded 2-dimensional submanifold (a one-sheeted hyperboloid for $c>0$, two-sheeted for $c<0$). $c=0$ is a critical value.

(b) Let $C=f^{-1}(0)$. Suppose $0$ has an open neighborhood $W\subseteq C$ with a homeomorphism $h:W\to\mathbb{R}^2$. Choose an open disc $D$ around $h(0)$; then $V=h^{-1}(D)$ is an open neighborhood of $0$ in $C$ and $V\setminus\lbrace0\rbrace\cong D\setminus\lbrace h(0)\rbrace$ is connected. Since $V$ is open in $C$ and contains $0$, it contains $(t,0,t)$ and $(t,0,-t)$ for all small $t>0$. But $z$ is continuous and nonzero on $C\setminus\lbrace0\rbrace$ (if $z=0$ then $x=y=0$), so it has constant sign on the connected set $V\setminus\lbrace0\rbrace$. Contradiction. So $C$ is not a topological manifold near $0$, and in particular not an embedded submanifold. $\square$

### 3. A 2-form on $\mathbb{R}^3$

(a) $d\omega=dx\wedge dy\wedge dz+dy\wedge dz\wedge dx+dz\wedge dx\wedge dy=3\,dx\wedge dy\wedge dz$, since cyclic permutations of three letters are even.

(b) Using $dx^i\wedge dx^j(u,v)=u^iv^j-u^jv^i$:
$$
\omega_p(u,v)=x(u^2v^3-u^3v^2)+y(u^3v^1-u^1v^3)+z(u^1v^2-u^2v^1)=p\cdot(u\times v).
$$

(c) $F^*\omega=h\,d\varphi\wedge d\theta$ with $h=(F^*\omega)(\partial_\varphi,\partial_\theta)=\omega_F(F_\varphi,F_\theta)=F\cdot(F_\varphi\times F_\theta)$. With
$F_\varphi=(\cos\varphi\cos\theta,\cos\varphi\sin\theta,-\sin\varphi)$ and $F_\theta=(-\sin\varphi\sin\theta,\sin\varphi\cos\theta,0)$:
$$
F_\varphi\times F_\theta=(\sin^2\varphi\cos\theta,\ \sin^2\varphi\sin\theta,\ \sin\varphi\cos\varphi),
$$
and $F\cdot(F_\varphi\times F_\theta)=\sin^3\varphi\cos^2\theta+\sin^3\varphi\sin^2\theta+\sin\varphi\cos^2\varphi=\sin\varphi$. So
$$
F^*\omega=\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\wedge d\theta. \qquad\square
$$

### 4. Brackets

Use $[X,Y]^j=X^i\partial_iY^j-Y^i\partial_iX^j$. Write $X=(-y,x)$, $Y=(1,0)$, $Z=(x,y)$.

- $[X,Y]$: $Y$ is constant so the first term vanishes; $Y^i\partial_iX=\partial_xX=(0,1)$. So $[X,Y]=-\partial_y$.
- $[X,Z]$: $X^i\partial_iZ=X$ (since $DZ=I$), and $Z^i\partial_iX=DX\cdot Z=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}=X$. So $[X,Z]=0$.

*Interpretation.* $X$ generates rotations $\theta_t$ and $Z$ generates dilations $\psi_s(p)=e^sp$. Rotations commute with scalings, consistent with $[X,Z]=0$ (Problem 21). The translations generated by $Y$ do not commute with rotations, and $[X,Y]\ne0$ records this. $\square$

### 5. The angle form

Write $\omega=P\,dy+Q\,dx$ with $P=x/r^2$, $Q=-y/r^2$, $r^2=x^2+y^2$. Then $d\omega=(\partial_xP-\partial_yQ)\,dx\wedge dy$ and
$$
\partial_xP=\frac{r^2-2x^2}{r^4}=\frac{y^2-x^2}{r^4},\qquad\partial_yQ=-\frac{r^2-2y^2}{r^4}=\frac{y^2-x^2}{r^4},
$$
so $d\omega=0$.

With $\gamma(t)=(\cos t,\sin t)$, $0\le t\le2\pi$: $\gamma^*\omega=(\cos t\cdot\cos t-\sin t\cdot(-\sin t))\,dt=dt$, so $\int_\gamma\omega=2\pi$. If $\omega=df$, then $\int_\gamma df=f(\gamma(2\pi))-f(\gamma(0))=0$. So $\omega$ is not exact.

On $\lbrace x>0\rbrace$, $\vartheta=\arctan(y/x)$ is smooth and $d\vartheta=\dfrac{1}{1+y^2/x^2}\left(\dfrac{dy}{x}-\dfrac{y\,dx}{x^2}\right)=\dfrac{x\,dy-y\,dx}{x^2+y^2}=\omega$. $\square$

### 6. $O(n)$

Let $\mathrm{Sym}(n)$ be the symmetric matrices, a vector space of dimension $n(n+1)/2$, and $\Phi:GL(n)\to\mathrm{Sym}(n)$, $\Phi(A)=A^TA$. Then $O(n)=\Phi^{-1}(I)$ and
$$
d\Phi_A(B)=\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_0(A+tB)^T(A+tB)=B^TA+A^TB.
$$
For $A\in O(n)$ and $C\in\mathrm{Sym}(n)$, put $B=\tfrac12AC$. Then $d\Phi_A(B)=\tfrac12C^TA^TA+\tfrac12A^TAC=\tfrac12C+\tfrac12C=C$. So $I$ is a regular value, and $O(n)$ is an embedded submanifold of dimension $n^2-\tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}=\tfrac{n(n-1)}{2}$, with
$$
T_IO(n)=\ker d\Phi_I=\lbrace B:B^T+B=0\rbrace,
$$
the skew-symmetric matrices (dimension $n(n-1)/2$, as it must be). $\square$

## Core

### 7. Orientability of $\mathbb{RP}^n$

Let $\mu=dx^0\wedge\dots\wedge dx^n$ on $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$, $N=x^i\partial_i$ the position field, and $\Omega=\iota^*(i_N\mu)$, where $\iota:S^n\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$. $\Omega$ is a volume form on $S^n$: for a basis $v_1,\dots,v_n$ of $T_xS^n=x^\perp$, $\Omega(v_1,\dots,v_n)=\mu(x,v_1,\dots,v_n)\ne0$.

Let $a(x)=-x$. As a linear map on $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$, $a^*\mu=\det(-I)\mu=(-1)^{n+1}\mu$, and $da_x(N_x)=-x=N_{-x}$, so $N$ is $a$-related to itself. Hence $a^*(i_N\mu)=i_N(a^*\mu)$, and restricting to the sphere,
$$
a^*\Omega=(-1)^{n+1}\Omega.
$$
Let $q:S^n\to\mathbb{RP}^n$ be the quotient, a smooth 2-sheeted covering and local diffeomorphism with $q\circ a=q$.

*$n$ odd.* Then $a^*\Omega=\Omega$. Define $\nu$ on $\mathbb{RP}^n$ by $\nu_{q(x)}=\big((dq_x)^{-1}\big)^*\Omega_x$ on a neighborhood where $q$ has a local inverse. The two choices $x,-x$ give the same form because $a^*\Omega=\Omega$ and $q\circ a=q$. So $\nu$ is a well-defined smooth nowhere-vanishing $n$-form and $\mathbb{RP}^n$ is orientable.

*$n$ even.* Suppose $\nu$ is a volume form on $\mathbb{RP}^n$. Then $q^*\nu$ is nowhere vanishing on $S^n$, so $q^*\nu=f\Omega$ with $f$ smooth and never zero; $S^n$ is connected, so $f$ has constant sign. From $q\circ a=q$, $a^*q^*\nu=q^*\nu$, i.e.
$$
(f\circ a)\,a^*\Omega=f\Omega\ \Longrightarrow\ -(f\circ a)\Omega=f\Omega\ \Longrightarrow\ f(-x)=-f(x),
$$
contradicting constant sign. So $\mathbb{RP}^n$ is not orientable. $\square$

### 8. The irrational line on the torus

Write $\gamma=p\circ\ell$ with $\ell(t)=(t,\alpha t)$ and $p:\mathbb{R}^2\to T^2$, $p(s,u)=(e^{2\pi is},e^{2\pi iu})$, a smooth covering map and local diffeomorphism. $d\ell$ is injective, so $d\gamma=dp\circ d\ell$ is injective: $\gamma$ is an immersion.

*Injective.* If $\gamma(s)=\gamma(t)$ then $s-t\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $\alpha(s-t)\in\mathbb{Z}$. If $s\ne t$, $\alpha=\alpha(s-t)/(s-t)\in\mathbb{Q}$, contradiction.

*Not an embedding.* The values $n\alpha\bmod1$, $n\in\mathbb{Z}$, are pairwise distinct (by irrationality) and dense, so every interval $(0,\varepsilon)$ contains infinitely many of them. Choose distinct integers $n_k$ with $n_k\alpha\bmod1\in(0,1/k)$; since they are distinct, $|n_k|\to\infty$ along a subsequence. Then
$$
\gamma(n_k)=(1,e^{2\pi in_k\alpha})\to(1,1)=\gamma(0).
$$
If $\gamma$ were a homeomorphism onto its image, we would get $n_k\to0$, which is false. $\square$

### 9. Flows

(a) Integral curves solve $\dot x=x^2$, $x(0)=x_0$. For $x_0=0$, $x\equiv0$. Otherwise separate variables: $-1/x=t-1/x_0$, so
$$
\theta_t(x_0)=\frac{x_0}{1-tx_0}.
$$
(Check: $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{x_0}{1-tx_0}=\frac{x_0^2}{(1-tx_0)^2}=\theta_t(x_0)^2$.) The maximal interval is where $1-tx_0>0$ (the solution starts there and blows up as $tx_0\to1$): $t<1/x_0$ for $x_0>0$, $t>1/x_0$ for $x_0<0$, all $t$ for $x_0=0$. So
$$
\mathcal{D}=\lbrace(t,x):tx<1\rbrace.
$$
Since $\mathcal{D}\ne\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$ (e.g. the curve through $x_0=1$ exists only for $t<1$), $X$ is not complete.

(b) $\dot x=x$, $\dot y=2y$ gives $\theta_t(x,y)=(e^tx,e^{2t}y)$, defined for all $t$. Then $\theta_s(\theta_t(x,y))=(e^se^tx,e^{2s}e^{2t}y)=\theta_{s+t}(x,y)$. $\square$

### 10. Lie derivative of $x\,dy$ along the rotation field

The flow of $X=x\partial_y-y\partial_x$ is rotation: $\theta_t(x,y)=(x\cos t-y\sin t,\ x\sin t+y\cos t)$ (check: $\frac{d}{dt}$ at $t=0$ is $(-y,x)$).

(a)
$$
\theta_t^*(x\,dy)=(x\cos t-y\sin t)\,(\sin t\,dx+\cos t\,dy).
$$
Differentiating at $t=0$: $(-y)(dy)+x(dx)$. So $\mathcal{L}_X\omega=x\,dx-y\,dy$.

(b) $i_X\omega=x\cdot dy(X)=x\cdot x=x^2$, so $d(i_X\omega)=2x\,dx$. And $d\omega=dx\wedge dy$, with $i_X(dx\wedge dy)=dx(X)\,dy-dy(X)\,dx=-y\,dy-x\,dx$. Cartan's formula:
$$
\mathcal{L}_X\omega=i_Xd\omega+d\,i_X\omega=(-x\,dx-y\,dy)+2x\,dx=x\,dx-y\,dy.
$$
The two computations agree. $\square$

### 11. $\int_{S^2}\omega=4\pi$

*Directly.* $F$ restricted to $(0,\pi)\times(0,2\pi)$ is a diffeomorphism onto $S^2$ minus a closed set of measure zero (a half great circle), which doesn't affect the integral. It is orientation-preserving for the Stokes orientation: the outward normal at $F$ is $F$ itself and $\det(F,F_\varphi,F_\theta)=F\cdot(F_\varphi\times F_\theta)=\sin\varphi>0$. So by Problem 3(c),
$$
\int_{S^2}\omega=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta=2\pi\cdot2=4\pi.
$$

*By Stokes.* $\int_{S^2}\omega=\int_{\overline{B}^3}d\omega=3\operatorname{vol}(B^3)=3\cdot\tfrac43\pi=4\pi$. $\square$

### 12. $H^1_{dR}(S^1)\cong\mathbb{R}$

Every 1-form on the 1-manifold $S^1$ is closed. Define $I:\Omega^1(S^1)\to\mathbb{R}$, $I(\omega)=\int_{S^1}\omega$ (counterclockwise orientation). $I$ is linear and kills exact forms (Stokes, or the fundamental theorem for line integrals), so it descends to $\bar I:H^1_{dR}(S^1)\to\mathbb{R}$.

*Surjective:* the restriction of the angle form of Problem 5 has integral $2\pi$.

*Injective:* let $\int_{S^1}\omega=0$ and $p:\mathbb{R}\to S^1$, $p(t)=(\cos t,\sin t)$. Then $p^*\omega=g(t)\,dt$ with $g$ smooth and $2\pi$-periodic, and $\int_0^{2\pi}g=\int_{S^1}\omega=0$. Let $F(t)=\int_0^tg$. Then $F(t+2\pi)-F(t)=\int_t^{t+2\pi}g=\int_0^{2\pi}g=0$, so $F=f\circ p$ for a function $f$ on $S^1$. $f$ is smooth because $p$ is a local diffeomorphism and $F$ is smooth. Finally $p^*(df)=dF=g\,dt=p^*\omega$, and since $p$ is a local diffeomorphism, $df=\omega$. So $[\omega]=0$. $\square$

### 13. Top-degree forms on closed manifolds

(a) Stokes' theorem with $\partial M=\emptyset$: $\int_Md\eta=\int_{\partial M}\eta=0$ (forms on a compact manifold are compactly supported).

(b) $M$ is oriented, so it has a positively oriented volume form $\mu$, and $\int_M\mu>0$ (it is positive in every oriented chart, and we assume $M\ne\emptyset$). By (a), $\mu$ is not exact. It is closed ($d\mu$ is an $(n+1)$-form on an $n$-manifold), so $[\mu]\ne0$ in $H^n_{dR}(M)$.

(c) $H^n_{dR}(S^n)\ne0$ by (b), while $H^n_{dR}(\mathbb{R}^n)=0$ by the Poincaré lemma ($n\ge1$). de Rham cohomology is a diffeomorphism invariant, so $S^n\not\cong\mathbb{R}^n$. The simpler reason: $S^n$ is compact and $\mathbb{R}^n$ is not, so they aren't even homeomorphic. $\square$

### 14. No submersions from compact manifolds to $\mathbb{R}^k$

Suppose $F:M\to\mathbb{R}^k$ is a smooth submersion. Submersions are open maps (by the rank theorem, $F$ is locally a linear projection, which is open). So $F(M)$ is open in $\mathbb{R}^k$. It is also compact, hence closed, and nonempty. $\mathbb{R}^k$ is connected, so $F(M)=\mathbb{R}^k$, which is not compact ($k\ge1$). Contradiction. $\square$

### 15. Existence of Riemannian metrics

Cover $M$ by charts $(U_\alpha,\varphi_\alpha)$ and let $g_\alpha=\varphi_\alpha^*\bar g$, the pullback of the Euclidean metric $\bar g=\sum(dx^i)^2$: a Riemannian metric on $U_\alpha$ (positive definite since $d\varphi_\alpha$ is injective). Let $\lbrace\psi_\alpha\rbrace$ be a smooth partition of unity subordinate to $\lbrace U_\alpha\rbrace$. Then $\psi_\alpha g_\alpha$, extended by zero outside $\operatorname{supp}\psi_\alpha$, is a smooth symmetric 2-tensor field on $M$, and
$$
g=\sum_\alpha\psi_\alpha g_\alpha
$$
is a locally finite sum, hence smooth and symmetric. For $p\in M$ and $0\ne v\in T_pM$, every term $\psi_\alpha(p)g_\alpha(v,v)$ is $\ge0$, and some $\psi_\alpha(p)>0$ (they sum to 1), with $p\in U_\alpha$ so $g_\alpha(v,v)>0$. So $g_p(v,v)>0$. $\square$

(The same argument fails for Lorentzian metrics: convex combinations of indefinite forms can be degenerate.)

### 16. Parallelizability

(a) $p:\mathbb{R}^2\to T^2$, $p(s,u)=(e^{2\pi is},e^{2\pi iu})$, is a local diffeomorphism and $p(x+m)=p(x)$ for $m\in\mathbb{Z}^2$. Since translations preserve $\partial_s,\partial_u$, the vector fields $E_1=dp(\partial_s)$, $E_2=dp(\partial_u)$ are well defined on $T^2$, smooth, and linearly independent at every point. (Alternatively: $T^2$ is a Lie group, and every Lie group is parallelizable by left-invariant fields.)

(b) Use $\langle p,q\rangle=\operatorname{Re}(p\bar q)$, the Euclidean inner product on $\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{R}^4$, and $|pq|=|p||q|$. For a unit quaternion $p$ and $u\in\lbrace i,j,k\rbrace$:
- $|up|=1$;
- $\langle up,p\rangle=\operatorname{Re}(up\bar p)=\operatorname{Re}(u)=0$, so $up\in p^\perp=T_pS^3$;
- $\langle ip,jp\rangle=\operatorname{Re}(ip\bar p\bar j)=\operatorname{Re}(i\cdot(-j))=\operatorname{Re}(-k)=0$; similarly $\langle ip,kp\rangle=\operatorname{Re}(-ik)=\operatorname{Re}(j)=0$ and $\langle jp,kp\rangle=\operatorname{Re}(-jk)=\operatorname{Re}(-i)=0$.

The maps $p\mapsto up$ are restrictions of linear maps, hence smooth. So $(ip,jp,kp)$ is a global orthonormal frame on $S^3$.

(c) A frame on $S^2$ would include a nowhere-vanishing vector field, which doesn't exist by the hairy ball theorem ([3.4 notes](../3.4-algebraic-topology/notes.md), §4.3). The quaternion trick works on $S^3$ (and the analogous complex trick on $S^1$) because these spheres are groups of unit elements in a normed division algebra; $S^2$ is not a Lie group, since every Lie group is parallelizable. $\square$

## Qualifying-exam level

### 17. $H^*_{dR}(S^n)$

Let $U=S^n\setminus\lbrace N\rbrace$, $V=S^n\setminus\lbrace S\rbrace$. Both are diffeomorphic to $\mathbb{R}^n$, so by the Poincaré lemma $H^0=\mathbb{R}$ and $H^k=0$ for $k\ge1$. $U\cap V\cong S^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}$ is homotopy equivalent to $S^{n-1}$, so by homotopy invariance $H^k(U\cap V)\cong H^k(S^{n-1})$.

*$n=1$.* $U\cap V$ is two disjoint intervals: $H^0(U\cap V)=\mathbb{R}^2$, $H^1=0$. The Mayer–Vietoris sequence reads
$$
0\to H^0(S^1)\to H^0(U)\oplus H^0(V)\xrightarrow{\ \beta\ }H^0(U\cap V)\xrightarrow{\ \delta\ }H^1(S^1)\to H^1(U)\oplus H^1(V)=0.
$$
$H^0(S^1)=\mathbb{R}$ (connected). Dimensions: $\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2\to H^1(S^1)\to0$. Exactness gives $\dim\operatorname{im}\beta=2-1=1$, so $\dim H^1(S^1)=2-1=1$. And $H^k(S^1)=0$ for $k\ge2$ by dimension.

*$n\ge2$.* All of $S^n,U,V,U\cap V$ are connected.
- Degree 0–1: $0\to\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^2\xrightarrow{\beta}\mathbb{R}\xrightarrow{\delta}H^1(S^n)\to0$. $\beta(a,b)=a-b$ is surjective, so $\delta=0$ and $H^1(S^n)=0$.
- For $k\ge2$: $0=H^{k-1}(U)\oplus H^{k-1}(V)\to H^{k-1}(U\cap V)\xrightarrow{\delta}H^k(S^n)\to H^k(U)\oplus H^k(V)=0$, so $H^k(S^n)\cong H^{k-1}(S^{n-1})$.

By induction from $n=1$: $H^k_{dR}(S^n)=\mathbb{R}$ for $k=0$ and $k=n$, and $0$ otherwise. $\square$

### 18. Smooth Brouwer

(a) Suppose $r:\overline{B}^n\to S^{n-1}$ is smooth with $r\circ\iota=\mathrm{id}$, where $\iota:S^{n-1}=\partial\overline{B}^n\hookrightarrow\overline{B}^n$. Let $\sigma$ be a volume form on $S^{n-1}$ positive for the Stokes orientation ($n-1\ge1$), so $\int_{S^{n-1}}\sigma>0$. Then
$$
0<\int_{S^{n-1}}\sigma=\int_{S^{n-1}}\iota^*r^*\sigma=\int_{\overline{B}^n}d(r^*\sigma)=\int_{\overline{B}^n}r^*(d\sigma)=0,
$$
since $d\sigma$ is an $n$-form on the $(n-1)$-manifold $S^{n-1}$, hence zero. Contradiction.

(b) Let $f:\overline{B}^n\to\overline{B}^n$ be smooth with $f(x)\ne x$ for all $x$. Put $u(x)=x-f(x)\ne0$ and look for $t(x)\ge0$ with $|x+t\,u(x)|=1$:
$$
t(x)=\frac{-(x\cdot u)+\sqrt{(x\cdot u)^2+|u|^2(1-|x|^2)}}{|u|^2}.
$$
The discriminant is positive on the open ball (second term $>0$). On the sphere $|x|=1$ it equals $(x\cdot u)^2$, and $x\cdot u=1-x\cdot f(x)\ge1-|f(x)|\ge0$, with equality only if $x\cdot f(x)=1$, which (Cauchy–Schwarz, $|x|=1$, $|f(x)|\le1$) forces $f(x)=x$. So $x\cdot u>0$ on the sphere, the discriminant is positive everywhere, and $t$ is smooth. On the sphere $t(x)=\frac{-(x\cdot u)+|x\cdot u|}{|u|^2}=0$. So $r(x)=x+t(x)u(x)$ is a smooth retraction $\overline{B}^n\to S^{n-1}$, contradicting (a). $\square$

(The continuous Brouwer theorem then follows by approximating a continuous map uniformly by smooth ones, e.g. via Stone–Weierstrass, and using compactness.)

### 19. Closed 1-forms on simply connected manifolds are exact

Let $\omega$ be closed. By the Poincaré lemma, every point has a coordinate ball $B$ on which $\omega=df_B$ for some smooth $f_B$.

*Step 1 (homotopy invariance of line integrals).* Let $\gamma_0,\gamma_1:[0,1]\to M$ be piecewise smooth paths from $p$ to $q$, and $H:I\times I\to M$ a continuous path homotopy ($H(\cdot,0)=\gamma_0$, $H(\cdot,1)=\gamma_1$, $H(0,s)=p$, $H(1,s)=q$). By the Lebesgue number lemma, subdivide $I\times I$ into small rectangles $R$, each mapped by $H$ into some ball $B_R$ with primitive $f_R$. For an edge $e$ of the grid from vertex $v$ to vertex $w$ lying in a rectangle $R$, set $c(e)=f_R(H(w))-f_R(H(v))$. If $e$ lies in two rectangles $R,R'$, then $f_R-f_{R'}$ has zero differential on $B_R\cap B_{R'}$, so it is constant on the connected set $H(e)$; hence $c(e)$ is independent of the choice of $R$. Summing $c$ around the boundary of a single rectangle gives $0$ (it telescopes with the single function $f_R$). Summing over all rectangles, interior edges cancel, so the sum of $c$ around $\partial(I\times I)$ is $0$. The vertical sides contribute $0$ (they map to the points $p$, $q$). On the bottom edge, each piece $\gamma_0|_{[t_j,t_{j+1}]}$ lies in $B_R$, where $\int\omega=\int df_R=c(e)$; so the bottom contributes $\int_{\gamma_0}\omega$, and likewise the top contributes $-\int_{\gamma_1}\omega$. Hence $\int_{\gamma_0}\omega=\int_{\gamma_1}\omega$.

*Step 2 (define the primitive).* Fix $p_0$. A connected manifold is connected by piecewise smooth paths (the set of points so reachable from $p_0$ is open and closed, using coordinate balls). Define $f(p)=\int_\gamma\omega$ for any piecewise smooth $\gamma$ from $p_0$ to $p$. Since $M$ is simply connected, any two such paths are path-homotopic, so by Step 1 $f$ is well defined.

*Step 3 ($df=\omega$).* Fix $p$ and a ball $B\ni p$ with $\omega=dg$ on $B$. For $q\in B$, concatenate a path to $p$ with a path inside $B$ from $p$ to $q$: $f(q)=f(p)+g(q)-g(p)$. So $f=g+\text{const}$ on $B$, hence $f$ is smooth and $df=dg=\omega$ there. $\square$

### 20. A non-integrable distribution

(a) $\alpha=dz-y\,dx$ satisfies $\alpha(V)=y-y=0$ and $\alpha(W)=0$. $\alpha$ is nowhere zero, so $\ker\alpha$ has rank 2, and $V,W$ are everywhere linearly independent and lie in it. So $D=\ker\alpha$.

(b) For any $f$,
$$
[V,W]f=(\partial_x+y\partial_z)(f_y)-\partial_y(f_x+yf_z)=f_{xy}+yf_{zy}-f_{xy}-f_z-yf_{zy}=-f_z,
$$
so $[V,W]=-\partial_z$. Since $\alpha(-\partial_z)=-1\ne0$, $[V,W]\notin D$ at every point. $D$ is not involutive. An integrable distribution is involutive (if $S$ is an integral surface, $V,W$ are tangent to $S$, so their bracket is tangent to $S$), so $D$ is not integrable.

(c) $d\alpha=-dy\wedge dx=dx\wedge dy$ and $\alpha\wedge d\alpha=dz\wedge dx\wedge dy=dx\wedge dy\wedge dz\ne0$.

*Why it detects (b).* For sections $V,W$ of $\ker\alpha$, $d\alpha(V,W)=V(\alpha(W))-W(\alpha(V))-\alpha([V,W])=-\alpha([V,W])$. So $\ker\alpha$ is involutive iff $d\alpha$ vanishes on $\ker\alpha$. Choosing $T$ with $\alpha(T)=1$, $(\alpha\wedge d\alpha)(T,V,W)=\alpha(T)\,d\alpha(V,W)=d\alpha(V,W)$ (the other terms contain $\alpha(V)$ or $\alpha(W)$). So $\ker\alpha$ is involutive iff $\alpha\wedge d\alpha=0$. Here $d\alpha(V,W)=1$, matching $-\alpha([V,W])=-\alpha(-\partial_z)=1$. $\alpha$ is the standard **contact form** on $\mathbb{R}^3$. $\square$

### 21. Commuting flows

($\Rightarrow$) Assume $[X,Y]=0$. Fix $p$ and set $V(t)=d(\theta_{-t})_{\theta_t(p)}\big(Y_{\theta_t(p)}\big)\in T_pM$. By the given formula, $V'(t_0)=d(\theta_{-t_0})\big([X,Y]_{\theta_{t_0}(p)}\big)=0$ for all $t_0$, so $V(t)=V(0)=Y_p$. Applying $d\theta_t$: $d(\theta_t)_p(Y_p)=Y_{\theta_t(p)}$ for all $p,t$, i.e. $Y$ is $\theta_t$-invariant.

Now fix $t$ and $q$, and let $c(s)=\theta_t(\psi_s(q))$. Then
$$
c'(s)=d\theta_t\big(Y_{\psi_s(q)}\big)=Y_{\theta_t(\psi_s(q))}=Y_{c(s)},
$$
so $c$ is an integral curve of $Y$ with $c(0)=\theta_t(q)$. By uniqueness of integral curves, $c(s)=\psi_s(\theta_t(q))$. So $\theta_t\circ\psi_s=\psi_s\circ\theta_t$.

($\Leftarrow$) If the flows commute, differentiate $\theta_t(\psi_s(q))=\psi_s(\theta_t(q))$ in $s$ at $s=0$: $d\theta_t(Y_q)=Y_{\theta_t(q)}$ for all $t,q$. Then $V(t)=d(\theta_{-t})(Y_{\theta_t(p)})=Y_p$ is constant, so $[X,Y]_p=\mathcal{L}_XY(p)=V'(0)=0$. $\square$

### 22. $SL(n,\mathbb{R})$ and $\det\exp=\exp\operatorname{tr}$

(a) $\det(A+tB)=\det(A)\det(I+tA^{-1}B)$. For any matrix $C$, $\det(I+tC)=1+t\operatorname{tr}C+O(t^2)$ (expand the determinant: the only terms of order $\le1$ in $t$ come from the product of diagonal entries). So
$$
d(\det)_A(B)=\frac{d}{dt}\Big|_0\det(A+tB)=\det(A)\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}B).
$$

(b) For $A\in SL(n)$ and $c\in\mathbb{R}$, $d(\det)_A(cA/n)=\operatorname{tr}(cI/n)=c$. So $d(\det)_A$ is onto $\mathbb{R}$ and $1$ is a regular value of $\det:GL(n)\to\mathbb{R}\setminus0$. Therefore $SL(n)=\det^{-1}(1)$ is an embedded submanifold of dimension $n^2-1$, and $T_ISL(n)=\ker d(\det)_I=\lbrace B:\operatorname{tr}B=0\rbrace$.

(c) Let $g(t)=\det(e^{tB})$. Since $\frac{d}{dt}e^{tB}=Be^{tB}$, the chain rule and (a) give
$$
g'(t)=\det(e^{tB})\operatorname{tr}\big(e^{-tB}Be^{tB}\big)=g(t)\operatorname{tr}B,
$$
using $\operatorname{tr}(P^{-1}BP)=\operatorname{tr}B$. With $g(0)=1$, $g(t)=e^{t\operatorname{tr}B}$; at $t=1$, $\det e^B=e^{\operatorname{tr}B}$. In particular $\operatorname{tr}B=0$ implies $\det e^B=1$, so $\exp$ maps $\mathfrak{sl}(n,\mathbb{R})$ into $SL(n,\mathbb{R})$. $\square$
