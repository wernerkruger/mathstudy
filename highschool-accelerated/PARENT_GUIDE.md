# Parent guide

[← Back to the track overview](README.md)

This guide is for the parent who runs this track with a mathematically gifted child. It covers how to support him well, how to work with the school, the competitions calendar, how to help if you know university mathematics yourself, and a sample weekly rhythm.

The short version: **go deep before you go fast, keep it fun, praise how he works rather than how clever he is, and agree the plan with the school.**

---

## 1. Supporting a gifted child

### Depth over speed

The aim of this track is not to finish school mathematics as early as possible. The aim is a child who understands mathematics deeply, enjoys it, and can think his way through problems he has never seen. Speed is a side effect.

In practice:

- **Proofs and "why" before shortcuts.** Every module explains why things are true. If he can use a rule but can't say why it works, he hasn't finished the topic.
- **Hard problems over many easy ones.** Ten routine exercises he can already do teach him little. One problem that takes 40 minutes and three wrong attempts teaches him a lot. Every module has a *Challenge* section for this.
- **Return to old topics.** Revisit earlier modules with harder problems and with the [olympiad](olympiad/) material. A topic is learned when he can use it in an unfamiliar setting months later.
- **Don't skip stages because he "already knows it".** Test with the problems first. If he can do the challenge problems and explain the proofs, move on. If he can only do the practice problems, stay.

### Enrichment versus acceleration

These are two different things, and he needs both.

- **Acceleration** means doing the regular curriculum earlier: klas 2 algebra in groep 8, calculus in klas 2, the central exam (*centraal examen*, CE) early. It saves boredom at school, but on its own it just moves the same material forward.
- **Enrichment** means going sideways and deeper: number theory, combinatorics, geometry proofs, puzzles, the history of mathematics, programming, competition problems. It doesn't race ahead in the school curriculum, but it builds the problem-solving skill that matters most later.

This track deliberately mixes both. The modules accelerate; the *Challenge* sections, "going further" boxes and the olympiad track enrich. If you must choose, **choose enrichment**. A child two years ahead who can only do routine exercises is worse off than a child one year ahead who loves hard problems.

### Keeping it fun

He should look forward to maths time. Some ways to keep it that way:

- Let him pick the order of challenge problems, and let him skip one that bores him.
- Mix in puzzles, games (Set, Nim, Hex, chess problems), and books such as *The Number Devil* (Hans Magnus Enzensberger) or *The Joy of x* (Steven Strogatz).
- Keep a "favourite problems" log in [PROGRESS.md](PROGRESS.md). Revisiting a beautiful problem is a reward in itself.
- Short sessions are fine. Two focused 45-minute sessions beat one tired 3-hour block.
- Some weeks will be light: school tests, illness, holidays, football. That is normal. The schedule has slack in it.

### Perfectionism and the "genius" identity

Gifted children often meet real difficulty for the first time later than other children, sometimes not until upper school or university. If his identity is "I'm the one who finds maths easy", then the first hard problem feels like a threat to who he is. Typical signs:

- refusing to try problems he might get wrong;
- being upset by any mark below 10;
- wanting to look up the answer quickly instead of staying stuck;
- avoiding competitions because he might not win.

What helps:

- **Make being stuck normal.** Say often that being stuck is what doing mathematics feels like, for everyone, including professional mathematicians. Talk about times you were stuck.
- **Give him problems he can't do yet**, on purpose and regularly, so struggle becomes ordinary.
- **Value attempts.** A page of failed ideas that ends in a partial solution is good work. Say so.
- **Don't introduce him as a genius**, and don't let family do it either. Talk about what he does ("he loves number puzzles") rather than what he is.

### Praise effort and strategy, specifically

General praise ("you're so smart") teaches him that ability is fixed and that he must protect it. Specific praise about how he worked teaches him that his methods matter. Compare:

| Instead of | Try |
|---|---|
| "You're a natural." | "You tried three small cases before guessing the pattern. That's exactly how mathematicians work." |
| "That was easy for you." | "That one looked easy, so let's find one that makes you think." |
| "Well done, all correct." | "Your proof of the divisibility rule for 9 is really clear. I can follow every step." |
| "Don't worry, you're clever." | "You've been stuck for 20 minutes. What have you tried? What else could you try?" |

Praise is most useful when it names the strategy: drawing a picture, trying a smaller case, working backwards, checking the answer, writing a clean proof.

### Avoiding burn-out

Signs to watch for: he stops asking maths questions on his own; he negotiates to do less; he does the work but without interest; sleep or mood change; he says "I have to" instead of "I want to".

If you see these, **cut the load first** and talk second. Drop to one or two short sessions a week of pure fun problems for a month. The track can wait. A child who still loves mathematics at 18 is the whole point; a child who finished calculus at 13 and hates it is not.

Guard his free time: unstructured play, sport, music, friends and boredom all matter. This track is planned at about 4 hours a week. Don't let it grow to 10.

### The social side

- **Find peers.** Being the only one who loves maths is lonely. Competitions, olympiad training days, a plusklas (see below) and maths camps put him with children like him. This is often the most valuable part of competitions.
- **School friends matter too.** Avoid timetabling that takes him out of everything social at school.
- **Modesty without hiding.** Help him talk about maths without showing off ("I'm doing a project on prime numbers") and without pretending to be average.
- **Gymnasium helps.** Latin and Greek give him another hard, interesting subject, and gymnasium classes usually contain several strong pupils.

## 2. Working with the school

### In groep 8 (2026–27)

His primary school will cover groep 8 *rekenen* (arithmetic). Stage A of this track overlaps with it and goes further. Tell the groep 8 teacher what he is doing at home. Ask whether he can:

- skip the groep 8 rekenen work he has mastered (shown by a test), and use that time for this track or a plusklas;
- take part in the W4Kangoeroe (wizSMART, or the harder wizBRAIN paper) in March 2027.

Many primary schools have a *plusklas* (pull-out group for gifted pupils) or a *verrijkingsgroep*. Ask about it.

### From the brugklas (klas 1, September 2027)

Ask for a meeting in the first weeks with his **mentor** and a member of the **wiskundesectie** (maths department), ideally the teacher who leads it. Bring:

- a short summary of what he has done (the [PROGRESS.md](PROGRESS.md) table is enough);
- two or three samples of his written work, including a proof;
- a clear, modest request.

Things to ask for, from most to least common:

1. **Compacting** (*compacten*): he does a short version of each chapter (the core exercises and the chapter test) and spends the saved lesson time on harder work. Many schools already do this for strong pupils.
2. **Working ahead in class** (*vooruitwerken*): permission to work on this track, or on later chapters of the school method, during maths lessons after finishing the compacted work.
3. **A plusklas or verrijkingsprogramma**: many gymnasia have one, sometimes cross-subject.
4. **Testing out**: sitting the chapter test without doing the chapter, and moving on if he passes.
5. **Contact for competitions**: a teacher who runs the Wiskunde Olympiade first round and the Kangoeroe at school.

Be patient. Schools are more open when the request is small, specific and comes with evidence. Revisit it each year. Avoid asking for a skipped year in maths only: timetables rarely allow it, and the track already gives him the acceleration.

### Early central exam (klas 4 or 5)

Under Dutch law (WVO 2020, article 2.56, paragraph 4) a school may let a VWO pupil sit the central exam in a subject one or two years early, that is, in **klas 4 or klas 5**. Conditions:

- the school and the subject teacher must both agree;
- the school exam (*schoolexamen*, SE) for that subject must be finished first;
- the result lapses if he is not promoted to the next year.

He will follow the new exam programmes, which start in klas 4 in September 2029. The first central exam under the new programme is probably in May 2032 (this is an inference from the timeline, not an official date). So **klas 5, May 2032, is the realistic early-exam date**. Raise it with the school in klas 4, once you know what the new programme and its school exam look like. The [track README](README.md) has the details.

An early CE is optional. The benefit is free time in klas 6 for a pre-university programme (see [RESOURCES.md](RESOURCES.md)). The risk is a rushed school exam. Decide in klas 4.

How to treat competitions:

- **They are for fun and for meeting peers, not for proving anything.** Agree this with him before each one.
- Before a competition, do two or three past papers together, untimed first.
- After it, go through the problems he missed. That is where most of the learning happens.
- Record each competition in the competition log in [PROGRESS.md](PROGRESS.md), including what he found hard, not only the score.

## 4. If you know university mathematics

You can be a great help, or you can accidentally take over. Some guidelines.

**Do:**

- **Ask questions instead of giving answers.** "What happens for small numbers?" "Can you draw it?" "What do you know about this kind of object?" "Is there a simpler problem you can solve?" (These are Pólya's questions from *How to Solve It*; the book is worth reading yourself.)
- **Let him stay stuck.** Agree a rule, for example: at least 20 minutes of honest effort before a hint, and hints come one at a time, smallest first.
- **Read his proofs critically and kindly.** Point to the line you don't follow, and ask him to fix it. Don't rewrite it for him.
- **Show him your own thinking**, including wrong turns, when you solve a problem together. He learns more from watching you be stuck and recover than from a polished solution.
- **Use the Dutch terms.** School and exams will use them. The [glossary](GLOSSARY.md) helps.
- **Check his school exam style.** Dutch exams reward exact answers, clear working and specific phrasings ("toon aan", "bereken exact"). Make sure he can write for that reader too.

**Don't:**

- **Don't jump ahead to "the real way".** Showing a 12-year-old modular arithmetic notation or a slick university proof can kill his own discovery. Let him find a clumsy method first; show the elegant one afterwards as a gift.
- **Don't fill silences.** A quiet child may be thinking.
- **Don't turn every conversation into a lesson.** Not every question at dinner needs a proof.
- **Don't mark everything.** Self-checking with [solutions.md](A-groep8-foundations/) builds independence. Spot-check instead.
- **Don't compare him with yourself at his age**, or with siblings or other children.
- **Don't let your enthusiasm set the pace.** If you're more excited about the track than he is, slow down.

A useful split: he works alone for most of the week, and you have one 30–45-minute session together to discuss a challenge problem, read a proof aloud, or plan the next week.

## 5. A sample weekly rhythm (about 4 hours)

This is a guide, not a timetable. Adjust it to school, sport and energy levels.

| Day | Time | What |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | 45 min | Read the next part of `notes.md`. Work through the worked examples himself before reading the solution. |
| Tuesday | 45 min | Practice problems from `problems.md`. Check with `solutions.md`. |
| Wednesday | — | Free. |
| Thursday | 45 min | School-exam-level problems. Optional: a WiskundeAcademie or Khan Academy video on the topic in Dutch. |
| Friday | — | Free. |
| Saturday | 60 min | One or two challenge problems. Unhurried. Write up one solution neatly. |
| Sunday | 30–45 min | With a parent: talk through the challenge problem, read one proof aloud, update [PROGRESS.md](PROGRESS.md), and choose a "favourite problem" if there was one. |

From klas 1, add about 1 hour a week of [olympiad](olympiad/) problems, for example by replacing part of Saturday with a past Kangoeroe or Olympiade paper.

In weeks with school tests, drop to Saturday and Sunday only. In holidays, either rest completely or do one fun project (a long investigation, a maths book, a programming project).

## 6. Further reading for parents

- George Pólya, *How to Solve It*. The classic on problem-solving heuristics.
- Carol Dweck, *Mindset*. On fixed versus growth mindsets; read critically, the core idea about praise is useful.
- Paul Lockhart, *A Mathematician's Lament*. A short essay on what school mathematics often misses.
- The Art of Problem Solving (AoPS) curriculum overview, useful for seeing how an American programme for gifted pupils orders the same material: <https://artofproblemsolving.com/store/list/aops-curriculum>.
