### 16.

(c) Construction as in notes §9. $P$ on the perpendicular bisector: reflecting in the bisector maps $A$ to $B$ and keeps $P$ fixed, and reflection keeps lengths, so $PA = PB$. (d) **30°:** construct an equilateral triangle (60°) and bisect one of its angles. **45°:** construct a perpendicular (90°) and bisect the right angle.

### 17.

(a) **9.** From each of the 6 corners there are 3 diagonals (to the corners that are not itself and not its two neighbours), giving $6\cdot 3 = 18$, but each diagonal has been counted from both ends: $18 \div 2 = 9$.

(b) Same argument: from each of the $n$ corners, $n - 3$ diagonals go out (all corners except itself and its two neighbours). That's $n(n - 3)$, and each diagonal is counted twice, so $\dfrac{n(n-3)}{2}$.

(c) $\dfrac{10\cdot 7}{2} = 35$.

### 20.

## Challenge

### 21.

$MA = MB = MC$ (all radii). So triangles $AMC$ and $BMC$ are isosceles.

- In triangle $AMC$, the base angles at $A$ and $C$ are equal; call them $x$.
- In triangle $BMC$, the base angles at $B$ and $C$ are equal; call them $y$.

$\angle ACB$ is made of the two angles at $C$: $\angle ACB = x + y$.

The angles of triangle $ABC$ are $x$ (at $A$), $y$ (at $B$) and $x + y$ (at $C$). So $x + y + (x + y) = 180°$, $2(x + y) = 180°$, and $\angle ACB = x + y = 90°$. $\square$

(This is the first theorem of geometry with a known discoverer: Thales of Miletus, around 600 BC.)

### 22.

(a) At each corner the interior and exterior angle are on a straight line, so they add up to $180°$. Over all $n$ corners: (interior sum) + (exterior sum) $= n\cdot 180°$. The interior sum is $(n - 2)\cdot 180°$, so the exterior sum is $n\cdot 180° - (n - 2)\cdot 180° = 2\cdot 180° = 360°$. $\square$

(b) Exterior angle $180° - 140° = 40°$; $360 \div 40 = 9$ sides.

### 23.

Where copies meet at a corner, the angles there must add up to exactly $360°$. All the angles are the interior angle $\alpha_n = 180° - \tfrac{360°}{n}$ of the regular $n$-gon, so $\tfrac{360°}{\alpha_n}$ must be a whole number $k$ (the number of polygons meeting at a corner).

- $n = 3$: $\alpha = 60°$, $k = 6$ ✓.
- $n = 4$: $\alpha = 90°$, $k = 4$ ✓.
- $n = 5$: $\alpha = 108°$, $360 \div 108 = 3.33\ldots$ ✗.
- $n = 6$: $\alpha = 120°$, $k = 3$ ✓.
- $n \ge 7$: $\alpha_n = 180° - \tfrac{360°}{n}$ is more than $120°$ (since $\tfrac{360}{n} < 60$) and less than $180°$. Then $k = \tfrac{360°}{\alpha_n}$ lies strictly between $2$ and $3$, so it is not a whole number ✗.

So only $n = 3, 4, 6$ are possible, and these three really do tile the plane (think of graph paper, a honeycomb, and a triangular grid). $\square$

### 24.

(a)
- **3 red faces:** the corner cubes: **8**.
- **2 red faces:** on an edge but not a corner. Each of the 12 edges has $3 - 2 = 1$ such cube: **12**.
- **1 red face:** in the middle of a face: **6** (one per face).
- **0 red faces:** the centre cube: **1**.

Check: $8 + 12 + 6 + 1 = 27$ ✓.

(b) For $n\times n\times n$:
- 3 faces: $8$.
- 2 faces: $12(n - 2)$.
- 1 face: $6(n - 2)^2$.
- 0 faces: $(n - 2)^3$ (the inner cube).

Check: with $m = n - 2$, $8 + 12m + 6m^2 + m^3 = (m + 2)^3$, which you can verify by expanding $(m + 2)^3 = (m + 2)(m^2 + 4m + 4) = m^3 + 6m^2 + 12m + 8$ ✓. So the four numbers add up to $n^3$.

### 25.

Put coordinates on it (this helps the picture): triangle 1 has its right angle at $(0, 0)$ and corners $(b, 0)$ and $(0, a)$. Triangle 2 has its right angle at $(0, a + b)$ and corners $(0, a)$ and $(a, a + b)$. The trapezium has corners $(0, 0)$, $(b, 0)$, $(a, a + b)$, $(0, a + b)$: its parallel sides (bottom and top) are $b$ and $a$, and its height is $a + b$.

(a) At the point $(0, a)$ three angles lie along the vertical side: the angle of triangle 1 at that point, the angle of the third triangle, and the angle of triangle 2. The angles of triangles 1 and 2 at this point are the **two different acute angles** of the original right-angled triangle (in triangle 1 it is the angle opposite the leg $b$, in triangle 2 the angle opposite the leg $a$), so they add up to $90°$. The three angles together make $180°$, so the angle of the third triangle is $90°$. $\square$

(b) **Trapezium formula:** $\tfrac12(a + b)(a + b) = \tfrac12(a + b)^2$.
**Three triangles:** $\tfrac12 ab + \tfrac12 ab + \tfrac12 c^2$ (the third triangle is right-angled with both legs $c$).

So $\tfrac12(a + b)^2 = ab + \tfrac12 c^2$. Multiply by 2: $a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = 2ab + c^2$, so $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$. $\square$

### 27.

### 28.

(a) Big square: $10^2 = 100$. The circle has diameter 10, so radius 5: area $25\pi \approx 78.5$. The small square has its diagonal equal to the diameter, 10. A square with side $s$ has diagonal $s\sqrt2$ (Pythagoras: $s^2 + s^2 = d^2$), so $s^2 = \tfrac{d^2}{2} = 50$: area 50.

(Neat: the small square has exactly half the area of the big one. Rotate the small square by 45° and you can see it: its corners touch the midpoints of the big square's sides.)

(b) $\dfrac{25\pi}{100} = \dfrac{\pi}{4} \approx 0.785$, about 78.5%. It doesn't depend on the size: for a square of side $s$, the circle has area $\pi\left(\tfrac{s}{2}\right)^2 = \tfrac{\pi}{4}s^2$, always $\tfrac{\pi}{4}$ of the square.

### 29.

(a) Join the centre $M$ to all six corners. This makes six triangles with two sides $r$ and an angle of $\tfrac{360°}{6} = 60°$ at $M$. They are isosceles with top angle $60°$, so the base angles are $60°$ too: they are equilateral. So each hexagon side is $r$, and the perimeter is $6r$.

The hexagon lies inside the circle, and each side is a straight line, shorter than the arc of the circle between the same two corners. So $6r < 2\pi r$, giving $\pi > 3$.

(b) The square around the circle has side $2r$ (the diameter), so perimeter $8r$. The circle lies inside the square, and its circumference is shorter than the square's perimeter: $2\pi r < 8r$, so $\pi < 4$. (That a convex curve inside another is shorter is intuitively clear; it can be made rigorous later.)

(c) Join $M$ to the six corners of the outer hexagon: again six equilateral triangles, but now the **height** of each (from $M$ to the touching point in the middle of a side) is $r$. In an equilateral triangle with side $s$, the height splits it into two right-angled triangles with hypotenuse $s$ and short leg $\tfrac{s}{2}$, so

$$
r^2 = s^2 - \left(\tfrac{s}{2}\right)^2 = \tfrac34 s^2, \qquad s = \frac{2r}{\sqrt3}.
$$

The outer hexagon's perimeter is $6s = \dfrac{12r}{\sqrt3} = 4\sqrt3\, r$. The circle is inside it, so $2\pi r < 4\sqrt3\, r$, giving $\pi < 2\sqrt3 \approx 3.46$.

So $3 < \pi < 3.47$. Archimedes continued doubling the number of sides (12, 24, 48, 96) to get $3\tfrac{10}{71} < \pi < 3\tfrac{1}{7}$.
