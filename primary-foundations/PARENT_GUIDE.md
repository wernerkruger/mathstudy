# Parent guide: helping an anxious child with maths

[← Back to the track](README.md)

This guide is for both parents. Read it once before you start, and come back to the "bad day" and "talking about mistakes" sections when you need them.

**The short version:** keep sessions short, calm and mostly successful. Talk about *how* he worked something out. Never time him. Never compare him with his brother. If a session goes wrong, stop kindly and try again tomorrow.

---

## 1. What the research says (and how sure we can be)

Maths anxiety is a real, well-studied reaction: tension and worry that get in the way of doing maths. It is not the same as being bad at maths. Plenty of children who can do the work still freeze when they feel watched, rushed or judged.

| Finding | Source | How strong is it? |
|---|---|---|
| Children of maths-anxious parents learned less maths over a school year and became more anxious about maths, **but only when those parents helped often with homework**. When anxious parents helped rarely, there was no such effect. The authors suggest games, books and apps as a more positive way for these parents to engage. | Maloney, Ramirez, Gunderson, Levine & Beilock (2015), *Psychological Science*. [Press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/parents-math-anxiety-can-undermine-childrens-math-achievement.html) | One well-designed study of children in grades 1–2 in the US. It shows an association over a year, not proof of cause, and the children were younger than ours. Still, it is the clearest evidence we have that *how* parents help matters as much as *whether* they help. |
| Stress takes up working memory, which is the "mental desk space" we need for calculating. Timed tests are a common trigger. Number talks, strategies and games are recommended instead. | Boaler, *Fluency Without Fear*, youcubed. [Link](https://www.youcubed.org/evidence/fluency-without-fear/) | An advocacy paper that draws on research, not a systematic review. The working-memory point is well supported elsewhere. The strong claims about timed tests are plausible but debated. |
| Interventions that build maths **skill** tended to lower anxiety *and* raise performance. Interventions that only work on the anxiety (for example, cognitive behavioural approaches) lowered anxiety but did little for performance. | Balt, Börnert-Ringleb & Orbach (2022), systematic review, *Frontiers in Education*. [Link](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2022.798516/full) | A systematic review, but of a small and mixed set of studies. The authors say the picture is not yet clear. |
| A broad review of what we know about maths anxiety: it appears in primary school, it is linked to (but separate from) maths performance, and the link probably runs both ways: anxiety hurts performance and struggling feeds anxiety. | Dowker, Sarkar & Looi (2016), review, *Frontiers in Psychology*. [Link](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2016.00508/text) | A narrative review: a good overview, not a meta-analysis. |
| "Growth mindset" interventions had small effects on average, and the link between mindset and achievement was weak. | Sisk et al. (2018), two meta-analyses, *Psychological Science*. | Strong evidence that posters and pep talks about "a growth mindset" are not enough on their own. Specific praise for effort and strategy is still sensible. |
| Writing about worries for ten minutes before a test helped some students. | Ramirez & Beilock (2011), *Science*. | Shown with older students (high school and university), not with 10-year-olds. Don't rely on it for now. |

**What this means for us.** The best-supported plan is to build real skill in small, successful steps, with a calm adult, without time pressure. Confidence follows competence. That is what this track is designed to do.

## 2. Do and don't

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep sessions to 15–20 minutes, 4–5 days a week. | Do a long catch-up session at the weekend. |
| Start with something he will certainly get right. | Start with "the hard one we got wrong yesterday". |
| Aim for about 70–80% correct. If it is lower, make it easier. | Push on through a sheet that is going badly. |
| Ask "How did you work that out?" for right *and* wrong answers. | Only react when an answer is wrong. |
| Let him use fingers, paper, the number line, money, blocks. | Insist he does it "in his head". |
| Use the method the school uses (see each unit's "Parent in 2 minutes"). | Teach your own shortcut "because it's faster". |
| Praise the specific thing he did: "You split 47 into 40 and 7. Smart." | Say "You're so clever" or "See, it's easy!" |
| Stop while it's still going well. | Stop only when he's upset. |
| Play games: they count as maths. | Treat games as a reward for doing "real" maths. |

### Words to avoid, and what to say instead

| Instead of… | Try… |
|---|---|
| "This is easy." | "This one has a few steps. Let's take the first one." (If it *is* easy for him, he'll notice himself.) |
| "Come on, you know this." | "Let's work it out together. What do you know that's close?" |
| "No, that's wrong." | "Let's check that one. Talk me through it." |
| "Hurry up." | Nothing. Wait. |
| "Your brother could do this when he was your age." | Never. Not even as encouragement. |
| "I was never good at maths either." | "Maths sometimes felt hard for me too, and practice helped." |
| "You're a maths person / not a maths person." | "You've got better at this since last month." |

## 3. A note to both parents about your own attitude

Children notice tone, sighs and impatience far more than words.

**If maths makes you uneasy:** the Maloney study suggests the risk comes from frequent, tense homework help. Lean on the games and activities, use the structured sheets and answer files here, and let the material do the explaining. It is fine to say "Let's look at the answer sheet together."

**If you are very comfortable with maths:** this is a real strength, and it has its own risks.

- **Don't lecture.** Keep explanations to one or two sentences, then let him try. If you notice you have been talking for more than 30 seconds, stop and ask him a question.
- **Don't show the faster way.** A neat trick that is obvious to you can make him feel he was slow or stupid. Use his method and the school's method. If he asks for another way, offer it as "another idea", not "the better way".
- **Don't say "this is easy" or "obviously".** To an anxious child, "easy" means "and if you can't do it, there's something wrong with you".
- **Hide the speed.** Don't answer before he has finished thinking, and don't finish his sentences.
- **Never compare him with his older brother**, or with yourself as a child, even kindly ("You're good at different things"). Comparisons tell him he is being measured.
- **Enjoy his thinking.** Your real gift is curiosity. Use the puzzle corners: "Ooh, I don't know. What do you think?" Being genuinely interested in his ideas is worth more than any explanation.

It may help if the parent who feels more relaxed about the *child's* struggle does most of the weekday sessions, and the more mathematical parent leads games, puzzle corners and real-life maths (cooking, maps, money, train times).

## 4. How to talk about mistakes

Mistakes are information. They show what he is thinking, and that is the most useful thing you can see.

1. **Stay neutral.** Same voice, same face for right and wrong answers.
2. **Ask before you tell.** "How did you get that?" Very often he will spot the slip himself while explaining. Let him have that win.
3. **Find what was right.** "You lined up the tens and ones perfectly. The only thing is this exchange (*inwisselen*)."
4. **Fix one thing.** Pick the most important error. Leave the rest for another day.
5. **Do a similar one straight after**, a little easier, so the last experience of that idea is success.
6. **Normalise it.** "That's a really common mix-up. Lots of people do that." "Mistakes help your brain notice something new."
7. **Use "yet".** "You can't do these *yet*." Use it lightly and honestly. Not as a slogan.

**What to do if he says "I'm stupid" or "I'm bad at maths":** don't argue and don't brush it off ("Don't be silly!"). Acknowledge the feeling, then point to evidence:

> "It feels really frustrating right now. I get that. Remember in September when the 7 times table felt impossible? Look at your progress sheet now."

The [progress tracker](PROGRESS.md) is there exactly for this.

## 5. Session structure (15–20 minutes)

| Step | Time | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Warm-up | 2–3 min | Something he'll definitely get right: a quick game, a few Warm-up items, a "number of the day" chat. |
| 2. Main bit | 8–10 min | One activity or one practice set from the current unit. Talk about how. |
| 3. Talk / puzzle | 2–3 min | One "talk about it" prompt, or a look at the puzzle corner (optional, only if he's in the mood). |
| 4. Finish on a win | 1–2 min | End with an item he can do. Tick off the session, add to [PROGRESS.md](PROGRESS.md) if he's ready. |

Practical tips:

- Same time and place each day if you can. A predictable routine lowers anxiety.
- Not straight after school if he is tired. After a snack works well for many children.
- Phone away (yours too).
- Pencil and eraser, not pen. Rubbing out is allowed and normal.
- Let him choose sometimes: "Practice set or game today?"
- Don't mark with red crosses. Mark right answers with a tick; for others, put a small dot and look at them together.

## 6. What to do on a bad day

Some days will go badly. That's normal and it's fine.

**Signs:** tears, "I can't", going blank, getting silly, sudden tummy ache, refusing to start, staring at the page.

**What to do:**

1. **Stop the task.** Calmly. "Let's stop that one. It's not a good moment for it."
2. **Name it without judgement.** "Your brain feels full right now. That happens to everyone."
3. **Breathe.** Slow breathing for a minute, or a glass of water, or a short walk.
4. **Go back to something easy** if he is willing, such as a game he likes or one or two Warm-up items, so the day ends with a success. If he isn't willing, just end the session.
5. **Don't make it up later.** No extra session at the weekend to "catch up".
6. **Next day, go back a step.** Use an easier set or an activity rather than a practice sheet.

**If bad days are frequent:** the work is too hard, or the sessions are too long, or something else is going on (tiredness, school, friendships). Drop back a level for a week, cut the session to 10 minutes, and talk to the teacher.

## 7. Working with the teacher

The teacher sees him in class every day. Home works best when it matches school.

**At the start of the year, ask:**

- Which maths method (*rekenmethode*) does the school use? (For example *De wereld in getallen*, *Pluspunt*, *Getal & Ruimte Junior*, *Wizwijs* or *Semsom*.) Which block is the class on now?
- Which methods do they use for adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing in groep 6: column-by-column (*kolomsgewijs*) or the standard written method (*cijferend*)?
- Are there times-tables (*tafels*) tests, and are they timed? Could he do them untimed, or in a quiet spot?
- Which pupil-tracking tests (*leerlingvolgsysteem*, LVS) do they use, and when?
- How does he seem in maths lessons: anxious, quiet, avoiding?

**Ask for a Rekengesprek.** Cito's LVS for arithmetic (*rekenen-wiskunde*) has an optional structured conversation, the *Rekengesprek*, in which the teacher talks with a pupil about how he solves problems and how he feels about maths (*rekenbeleving*). Parents can ask the school for one. It can show the teacher where the anxiety sits and which strategies he already uses. ([Cito: rekenen-wiskunde in the LVS](https://cito.nl/onderwijs/primair-onderwijs/leerling-in-beeld-leerlingvolgsysteem/dit-volg-je/rekenen-wiskunde/)) If the school uses a different LVS, ask whether they can do a similar one-to-one conversation.

**Tell the teacher** what you're doing at home, so they can mention it positively ("I heard you've been playing maths games at home!") and so you don't accidentally teach a topic in a different way.

**Pre-teaching helps.** Ask what is coming in the next block and do that unit at home a week or two before. Meeting a topic for the second time in class is a big confidence boost.

The school's internal support coordinator (*intern begeleider*, IB'er) is the person to involve if you and the teacher think he needs more support.

## 8. Pupil-tracking tests (LVS / Cito) and the doorstroomtoets

### LVS tests in groep 6

Most schools test arithmetic twice a year (around January–February, the "midden" test, and around June, the "eind" test). The Cito LVS arithmetic test has no time limit, is about half bare sums (*kale sommen*) and half word or context problems (*contextopgaven*), and reports on the four domains: Numbers (*Getallen*), Ratios (*Verhoudingen*), Measurement and Geometry (*Meten en Meetkunde*) and Relationships (*Verbanden*). ([Cito](https://cito.nl/onderwijs/primair-onderwijs/leerling-in-beeld-leerlingvolgsysteem/dit-volg-je/rekenen-wiskunde/))

How to handle them calmly:

- **Don't prepare specially or drill test papers.** Normal sessions are the preparation.
- **Talk about them lightly.** "It's a check for the teacher so she knows what to teach next. It's not about you being good or bad."
- **Results are for the adults.** You don't need to share the level, score or band with him. If he asks, focus on what he's improved.
- **Look at growth, not position.** One test on one day is a snapshot. Look at his scores over several tests.
- **Use the domain breakdown.** If one domain is lower, match it to a unit here.
- **Ask the teacher what the result means** before you react. A lower score than expected may be an anxious day.

### The doorstroomtoets (groep 8, about January–February 2029)

This is the national end-of-primary test. Key points for now:

- It tests arithmetic, language accuracy and reading against fixed levels (1F, and 1S/2F).
- It comes **after** the school gives its provisional advice (*voorlopig advies*). If the test result is **higher**, the school must reconsider the advice. If it is **lower, the advice stays**. It can only help, not harm. ([Cito](https://cito.nl/onderwijs/primair-onderwijs/leerling-in-beeld-doorstroomtoets/), [VO-raad timeline](https://www.vo-raad.nl/nieuws/data-2026-2027-rondom-doorstroomtoets-en-aanmelding-in-het-vo))
- Five different tests are approved in 2026–27, and there may be a single national test by about 2029–30. Check again in groep 7.

**It is two years away. Don't mention it to him in groep 6.** When it comes up at school, the message is: "This is one more chance to show what you can do. Your advice is already decided, and this test can only make it higher."

## 9. When to look for extra help

Most anxious children improve with calm, regular practice. Look for more help if, after several months of good support at home and at school:

**Possible signs of dyscalculia (*dyscalculie*), a specific, persistent difficulty with numbers:**

- Still counting on fingers one by one for small sums such as 6 + 7, and not "just knowing" them.
- Little feel for the size of numbers: can't say whether 48 or 84 is bigger without effort, or where 500 goes on a 0–1000 line.
- Number facts (*tafels*, number bonds to 10) don't stick even with a lot of good practice: known one day, gone the next.
- Mixing up place value (writing 3006 for "three hundred and six") well after classmates have stopped.
- Real difficulty with telling the time, money or estimating.
- The gap with classmates grows despite extra help.

**Signs that the anxiety itself needs more help:**

- Regular tummy aches, headaches or tears before maths lessons or tests.
- Avoiding school on maths days.
- Anxiety spreading to other subjects or to school in general.

**What to do:**

1. **Talk to the teacher and the intern begeleider.** In the Netherlands, schools follow the national protocol for serious arithmetic problems and dyscalculia (*Protocol ERWD*). The school first gives extra, targeted help and keeps track of it. An assessment usually only follows if the problems persist despite that help (*hardnekkigheid*, "persistence").
2. **Assessment** is done by a qualified psychologist or educational specialist (*orthopedagoog* or *GZ-psycholoog*). As far as we know, it is usually **not** reimbursed in the way that severe dyslexia care is. Check the costs with the school and the provider first.
3. **For anxiety**, talk to the GP (*huisarts*) or the school's support staff.

A diagnosis isn't a label to fear. It can bring practical help such as extra time, a times-table card (*tafelkaart*) or a calculator in some situations.
