# 8.4 Word problems, step by step

[← Groep 8 overview](../README.md) · [Activities](activities.md) · [Practice](practice.md) · [Answers](answers.md) · [Parent guide](../../PARENT_GUIDE.md) · [Glossary](../../GLOSSARY.md)

## What and why

In groep 8 most maths questions come wrapped in a story: a school trip, a recipe, a timetable, a sale. Dutch school calls these *redactiesommen* or *verhaaltjessommen* (word problems), and on the doorstroomtoets about half the arithmetic questions are like this (*contextopgaven*). Cito's tracking test is built the same way: roughly half bare sums (*kale sommen*), half word problems.

For an anxious child, word problems are often the scariest part. The sum itself may be easy, but a long text makes it feel like there's a trap. This unit gives him **one calm routine** to use on every word problem, so that a long text becomes a set of small, safe steps. The goal is not speed. The goal is that he always knows what to do next.

## The five steps

Put this on a card he can keep next to him. Use the Dutch words his teacher uses if they differ.

| Step | English | Dutch | What he does |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Read** | *Lezen* | Read it all once, calmly. Then read the **question** (the last sentence, usually) again. Underline it. |
| 2 | **Picture** | *Voorstellen / tekenen* | Make a quick sketch, strip, number line or table. Cross out information he doesn't need. |
| 3 | **Plan** | *Plannen* | Say in words what he will calculate first, and what next. "First I find out how many…, then…". |
| 4 | **Calculate** | *Uitrekenen* | Do the sums: in his head, on paper, or with a calculator if allowed. |
| 5 | **Check** | *Controleren* | Read the question again. Does the answer fit? Right unit (€, km, minutes)? Is the size sensible? |

A short phrase to remember it: **"Read, picture, plan, calculate, check."**

## Parent in 2 minutes

**How school teaches it.** Dutch methods use "models" (*modellen*) to turn a story into maths: the strip (*strook*) for parts of a whole, the number line for time and distance, the ratio table for anything "per". Teachers often say "*Wat wordt er gevraagd?*" ("What is being asked?") and "*Wat weet je al?*" ("What do you know already?"). Your role is to ask the same calm questions, not to explain the answer.

**The three questions to use instead of helping:**

1. "What is the question asking for? Can you say it in your own words?"
2. "Can you draw it?"
3. "What could you work out first?"

If he's still stuck after these, do the **picture** step together, then let him do the rest. Avoid finishing the problem for him; the success is in him finishing.

**What makes word problems hard** is usually not the arithmetic. It's one of these: a long text that overloads working memory (stress makes this worse); a word that's unclear; extra information that isn't needed; a question that needs two steps; or an answer that needs rounding to make sense (you can't book 6,3 buses).

## Common stumbling blocks and what to say

| What you might see | What is probably going on | What to say or do |
|---|---|---|
| Grabs the numbers and does whatever sum comes to mind | Panic: wants to get it over with | "Stop, pencil down. What is the question? Say it to me." |
| Uses every number in the text | Thinks all information must be needed | "Test-makers sometimes add extra information. Which number don't we need?" |
| Answers 6,3 buses or 4,5 people | Calculated correctly, didn't think about meaning | "Can you have 0,3 of a bus? What would you really do?" |
| Gets the first step right and stops | Two-step problem; thought the first answer was it | "Read the question again. Is that what it asked for?" |
| Says "I don't get it" before starting | Anxiety, not inability | "Let's just do step 1 and 2 together. No sums yet." |
| Mixes up minutes and decimal hours | 1,5 hours vs 1 hour 50 minutes | Draw a clock or a time line. |

## "I can…" goals

- I can use the five steps (read, picture, plan, calculate, check) on a word problem.
- I can say in my own words what a question is asking.
- I can draw a strip, number line or table to show a story.
- I can spot information I don't need.
- I can solve a word problem that takes two or three steps.
- I can check whether my answer makes sense, including the unit.
- I can read information from a timetable or a table to answer a question.

## Suggested plan (3–4 weeks, 15–20 minutes a session)

This unit works best alongside other units: one word-problem session a week can continue right up to the doorstroomtoets.

| Week | Sessions |
|---|---|
| 1 | Make the five-steps card. Activity 1 (problem without numbers) and activity 2 (write your own). Practice Set A (plan only, no calculating). Work through the worked examples together. |
| 2 | Activity 3 (spot the extra). Practice Set B, then Set C. |
| 3 | Activity 4 (act it out). Practice Set D, one or two problems per session. |
| 4 | Activity 5 (timetable trip). Practice Set E. Update the [progress tracker](../../PROGRESS.md). |
