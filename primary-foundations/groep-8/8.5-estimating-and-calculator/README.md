# 8.5 Estimating and the calculator

[← Groep 8 overview](../README.md) · [Activities](activities.md) · [Practice](practice.md) · [Answers](answers.md) · [Parent guide](../../PARENT_GUIDE.md) · [Glossary](../../GLOSSARY.md)

## What and why

The new national goals for arithmetic and mathematics (*kerndoelen rekenen-wiskunde*, law since August 2026) put new weight on **choosing how to calculate**: in your head, on paper, or with a calculator (*rekenmachine*), and then **checking whether the answer makes sense** ([what changes](https://www.actualisatiekerndoelen.nl/rekenenwiskunde/updates/nieuwe-kerndoelen-rekenen-en-wiskunde-wat-verandert-er)). In groep 8 the calculator also arrives in class (LOWAN puts it at the start of groep 8).

This unit covers three linked skills:

- **Rounding (*afronden*):** making a number simpler, to the nearest ten, hundred, whole number or one decimal.
- **Estimating (*schatten*):** using rounded numbers to get a quick "about" answer.
- **Using a calculator well:** knowing when it helps, typing carefully, reading the screen, and checking the answer with an estimate.

For an anxious child this unit is a quiet confidence builder. Estimating says "you don't have to be exact to be right", and checking gives him a way to catch his own mistakes before anyone else does.

## Parent in 2 minutes

**Rounding.** Look at the digit just to the right of where you're rounding. 0–4: round down. 5–9: round up. 382 to the nearest hundred: the tens digit is 8, so 400. School often shows this on a number line: "Is 382 nearer to 300 or to 400?" That picture is more useful for an anxious child than the rule.

**Money rounding.** In Dutch shops, cash totals are rounded to the nearest 5 cents, because 1- and 2-cent coins are rarely used. This is a nice real-life example.

**Estimating.** Round each number to something friendly, then calculate. 6 × 49 ≈ 6 × 50 = 300. School calls this *schatten*. A good estimate doesn't have to be the closest possible, just easy and sensible.

**The calculator.** Three things to know:

1. **The point.** Calculators show 2.5, not 2,5. He needs to read "2.5" as "2,5" and "3.5" (in euros) as €3,50.
2. **Order of operations.** A simple calculator does 3 + 4 × 2 in the order you type (= 14). A scientific calculator, like the one used in secondary school, does the × first (= 11). Unit 8.7 covers this; for now, use brackets or do one step at a time.
3. **Interpreting answers.** 250 : 8 = 31.25 is right on the calculator, but if the question is "how many minibuses of 8 seats?", the answer is 32.

**The golden rule:** estimate first, calculate second, compare third. If the estimate and the answer are far apart, something went wrong, usually a typing slip.

**When to use which method** is a judgment, not a rule, and talking about it is the point. Roughly: easy numbers in your head (25 × 4); medium sums on paper (487 + 235); awkward numbers or many steps on the calculator (17,8% of €2 345). Ask the teacher which methods are allowed on the doorstroomtoets the school uses; calculator use can differ per test and per section.

## Common stumbling blocks and what to say

| What you might see | What is probably going on | What to say or do |
|---|---|---|
| Rounds 7,85 to 7,8 | Looking at the wrong digit, or "5 goes down" | "Which number is it nearer to on the line: 7,8 or 7,9? 7,85 is exactly halfway, and halfway goes up." |
| Trusts the calculator completely | Calculators feel like authority | "Calculators never make mistakes, but fingers do. What did you expect?" |
| Writes 31,25 buses | Didn't interpret the answer | "Can you have 0,25 of a bus? What would you really do?" |
| Feels estimating is "cheating" or "wrong" | Has learned that only exact answers count | "In real life, most maths is estimating. Pilots, builders and shop owners estimate all day." |
| Rushes the estimate and gets a wild one | Wants to get to the "real" answer | Keep estimates to friendly numbers: tens, hundreds, halves. |

## "I can…" goals

- I can round numbers to the nearest 10, 100, 1 000, whole number or one decimal.
- I can estimate an answer with friendly numbers before calculating.
- I can spot an answer that can't be right, and say why.
- I can choose whether to calculate in my head, on paper or with a calculator, and explain my choice.
- I can read a calculator answer correctly (point = comma, money, rounding).
- I can decide what a calculator answer means in the story (round up, round down, or keep).
