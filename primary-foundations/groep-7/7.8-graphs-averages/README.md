# 7.8 Graphs and averages

[← Groep 7](../README.md) · [Activities](activities.md) · [Practice](practice.md) · [Answers](answers.md) · [Parent guide](../../PARENT_GUIDE.md) · [Games](../../GAMES.md) · [Glossary](../../GLOSSARY.md)

## What and why

This unit is about reading information from tables and graphs: bar charts (*staafdiagram*), line graphs (*lijngrafiek*) and pie charts (*cirkeldiagram*); working out an average (*gemiddelde*); and, new in the national goals since 2026, looking critically at graphs (*kritisch denken*): noticing when a graph is drawn to make you believe something. This is the domain Relationships (*Verbanden*) on pupil-tracking tests. It's a good unit to end the year with: there's less calculating, lots of talking, and children who find sums stressful are often very good at spotting a "sneaky graph".

## Parent in 2 minutes

**Reading graphs.** School teaches a routine: read the title first, then what's on each axis (and the units), then the scale (does each line go up in 1s, 2s, 10s?), then answer the question. Encourage him to say the routine out loud. Most mistakes come from skipping the scale.

**Which graph when.** Bar chart: comparing groups (how children travel to school). Line graph: something changing over time (temperature through the day). Pie chart: parts of one whole (how a class is divided over favourite fruits). Being able to say *why* a graph type fits is part of the new goals.

**Average (*gemiddelde*).** "Add them all up and divide by how many there are." School often introduces it as *eerlijk verdelen* ("sharing fairly"): stacks of blocks of different heights are levelled out until they're all the same height. That height is the average. Let him do this with Lego towers before using the rule.

**Critical reading (*kritisch denken*).** The new goals ask children to notice when a graph is designed to carry a message. The main tricks to look for at this age:
- the vertical axis doesn't start at 0, so a small difference looks huge;
- uneven steps on an axis;
- pictures that grow in two directions (a bag twice as tall is also twice as wide, so it looks four times as big);
- very few people asked ("9 out of 10 children" when only 10 were asked);
- missing title, units or source.

## Common stumbling blocks and what to say

| What you might see | What to say or do |
|---|---|
| Reads the bar height without checking the scale (each square = 2) | "What does one line on this axis mean? Count the steps between 0 and 10." |
| Confuses "how many" with "how many more" | "Do they want the size of one bar, or the difference between two bars?" Underline the question word. |
| Average: forgets to divide, or divides by the wrong number | "How many numbers did you add? That's how many we share between." Go back to levelling Lego towers. |
| Thinks the average must be one of the numbers | "Let's level the towers. Is the new height one of the old heights?" |
| Believes any graph "because it's maths" | "Who made this graph? What do they want you to think?" Celebrate scepticism. |

## "I can…" goals

- I can read a bar chart, a line graph and a pie chart, and check the scale first.
- I can say which kind of graph fits which kind of information.
- I can draw a simple bar chart or line graph from a table.
- I can work out the average of a few numbers and explain what it means.
- I can spot when a graph might be misleading, and say why.
