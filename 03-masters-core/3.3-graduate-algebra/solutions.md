## Warm-up

### 1.

The map $\beta:\mathbb{Z}/m\times\mathbb{Z}/n\to\mathbb{Z}/d$, $(\bar a,\bar b)\mapsto\overline{ab}$, is well defined (if $a'=a+km$ then $a'b-ab=kmb\in d\mathbb{Z}$ since $d\mid m$; similarly in $b$) and $\mathbb{Z}$-bilinear. It induces a homomorphism $\tilde\beta:\mathbb{Z}/m\otimes\mathbb{Z}/n\to\mathbb{Z}/d$ with $\tilde\beta(1\otimes1)=1$, so $\tilde\beta$ is surjective.

On the other hand, $\bar a\otimes\bar b=ab(1\otimes1)$, so $T=\mathbb{Z}/m\otimes\mathbb{Z}/n$ is cyclic, generated by $1\otimes1$. Also $m(1\otimes1)=\bar m\otimes1=0$ and $n(1\otimes1)=1\otimes\bar n=0$. Write $d=um+vn$ (Bézout); then $d(1\otimes1)=0$. So $\lvert T\rvert\le d$. A surjection from a group of order at most $d$ onto $\mathbb{Z}/d$ is an isomorphism.

### 2.

*$\mathbb{Q}\otimes\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}=0$.* It suffices to kill pure tensors. Let $q\in\mathbb{Q}$ and $x\in\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ with $nx=0$, $n\ge1$. Then
$$
q\otimes x=\left(n\cdot\tfrac qn\right)\otimes x=\tfrac qn\otimes nx=\tfrac qn\otimes0=0 .
$$

*$\mathbb{Q}\otimes\mathbb{Q}\cong\mathbb{Q}$.* Multiplication $\mathbb{Q}\times\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{Q}$ is bilinear, giving $\mu:\mathbb{Q}\otimes\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{Q}$ with $\mu(q\otimes r)=qr$; it is surjective ($\mu(q\otimes1)=q$). For injectivity, take any element $\sum_iq_i\otimes r_i$ and write all $q_i=a_i/N$, $r_i=b_i/N$ over a common denominator $N$. Then $q_i\otimes r_i=a_ib_i\left(\tfrac1N\otimes\tfrac1N\right)$, and
$$
\tfrac1N\otimes\tfrac1N=\left(N\cdot\tfrac1{N^2}\right)\otimes\tfrac1N=\tfrac1{N^2}\otimes1 .
$$
So every element has the form $c\left(\frac1{N^2}\otimes1\right)=\frac{c}{N^2}\otimes1$, i.e. every element is $q\otimes1$ for some $q$. Since $\mu(q\otimes1)=q$, $\mu$ is injective.

### 3.

(a) A homomorphism $\varphi:\mathbb{Z}/m\to\mathbb{Z}/n$ is determined by $y=\varphi(\bar1)$, which must satisfy $my=0$ in $\mathbb{Z}/n$; conversely each such $y$ defines a homomorphism. So $\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}/m,\mathbb{Z}/n)\cong\lbrace y\in\mathbb{Z}/n:my=0\rbrace$. Now $my\equiv0\pmod n$ iff $\frac nd\mid\frac md y$ iff $\frac nd\mid y$ (as $\gcd(\frac md,\frac nd)=1$). So the solutions are the multiples of $n/d$ in $\mathbb{Z}/n$, a cyclic subgroup of order $d$.

(b) Let $\varphi:\mathbb{Q}\to\mathbb{Z}$ and $q\in\mathbb{Q}$. For every $n\ge1$, $\varphi(q)=n\varphi(q/n)$ is divisible by $n$. The only integer divisible by every $n$ is $0$.

### 4.

(a) Let $\iota:2\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}$ be the inclusion and $\psi:2\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}$, $\psi(2k)=k$. If $\mathbb{Z}$ were injective, $\psi$ would extend to $\Psi:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}$ with $2\Psi(1)=\Psi(2)=\psi(2)=1$, impossible in $\mathbb{Z}$.

(b) Baer's criterion: $I$ is injective iff every homomorphism from an ideal $\mathfrak{a}\subseteq A$ to $I$ extends to $A$. The ideals of $\mathbb{Z}$ are $n\mathbb{Z}$. If $n=0$ there is nothing to do. If $n\neq0$ and $\psi:n\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Q}$ has $\psi(n)=q$, define $\Psi:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Q}$ by $\Psi(1)=q/n$. Then $\Psi(nk)=kq=\psi(nk)$. So $\mathbb{Q}$ is injective. (The same argument shows every divisible group is injective.)

### 5.

$S_3$ has three conjugacy classes: $\lbrace1\rbrace$, the three transpositions, the two $3$-cycles. So there are three irreducible representations, with $\sum d_i^2=6$, forcing degrees $1,1,2$. The one-dimensional ones are the trivial and sign characters. The values of the $2$-dimensional character $\chi$ follow from column orthogonality with the identity column, $\sum_i\chi_i(1)\chi_i(g)=0$ for $g\neq1$:
- at $(12)$: $1-1+2\chi((12))=0$, so $\chi((12))=0$;
- at $(123)$: $1+1+2\chi((123))=0$, so $\chi((123))=-1$.

| | $1$ (1) | $(12)$ (3) | $(123)$ (2) |
|---|---|---|---|
| trivial | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| sign | 1 | −1 | 1 |
| standard $\chi$ | 2 | 0 | −1 |

(Class sizes in parentheses.) The permutation representation on $\mathbb{C}^3$ has character $\pi=(3,1,0)$ (number of fixed points). Then
$$
\langle\pi,\mathrm{triv}\rangle=\tfrac16(3+3\cdot1+0)=1,\quad\langle\pi,\mathrm{sign}\rangle=\tfrac16(3-3+0)=0,\quad\langle\pi,\chi\rangle=\tfrac16(6+0+0)=1 .
$$
So $\mathbb{C}^3\cong\mathrm{triv}\oplus\mathrm{standard}$: the line spanned by $(1,1,1)$ and the plane $x_1+x_2+x_3=0$.

### 6.

(a) By the universal property of $P$ applied to $p_1',p_2'$, there is a unique $u:P'\to P$ with $p_iu=p_i'$. Symmetrically there is a unique $v:P\to P'$ with $p_i'v=p_i$. Then $p_i(uv)=p_i$ for $i=1,2$. The identity $\mathrm{id}_P$ also satisfies $p_i\,\mathrm{id}_P=p_i$, so by the uniqueness part of the universal property of $P$ (applied to the pair $p_1,p_2$ themselves), $uv=\mathrm{id}_P$. Similarly $vu=\mathrm{id}_{P'}$.

(b) $A\otimes_\mathbb{Z}B$ is a commutative ring with $(a\otimes b)(a'\otimes b')=aa'\otimes bb'$, and $i_A(a)=a\otimes1$, $i_B(b)=1\otimes b$ are ring homomorphisms. Let $\varphi:A\to C$ and $\psi:B\to C$ be ring homomorphisms into a commutative ring $C$. The map $A\times B\to C$, $(a,b)\mapsto\varphi(a)\psi(b)$, is $\mathbb{Z}$-bilinear, so there is an additive $\theta:A\otimes B\to C$ with $\theta(a\otimes b)=\varphi(a)\psi(b)$. It is multiplicative on pure tensors,
$$
\theta(aa'\otimes bb')=\varphi(a)\varphi(a')\psi(b)\psi(b')=\theta(a\otimes b)\,\theta(a'\otimes b')
$$
(using commutativity of $C$), hence on all elements by distributivity, and $\theta(1\otimes1)=1$. Also $\theta i_A=\varphi$ and $\theta i_B=\psi$. Uniqueness: any ring map $\theta'$ with these properties satisfies $\theta'(a\otimes b)=\theta'((a\otimes1)(1\otimes b))=\varphi(a)\psi(b)$, and pure tensors generate $A\otimes B$ additively.

### 7.

*(a) ⇒ (c).* Define $\eta:M\to M'\oplus M''$, $\eta(m)=(r(m),g(m))$. Injective: if $r(m)=0$ and $g(m)=0$, then $m=f(x)$ for some $x$ (exactness), and $x=rf(x)=r(m)=0$, so $m=0$. Surjective: given $(x,y)$, pick $m_0$ with $g(m_0)=y$ and put $m=m_0+f(x-r(m_0))$. Then $g(m)=y$ (as $gf=0$) and $r(m)=r(m_0)+x-r(m_0)=x$. Let $\theta=\eta^{-1}$. Then $\eta(f(x))=(x,0)$, so $\theta(x,0)=f(x)$; and $g\theta(x,y)=y$ since the second coordinate of $\eta$ is $g$.

*(b) ⇒ (c).* Define $\theta(x,y)=f(x)+s(y)$. Then $g\theta(x,y)=gs(y)=y$ and $\theta(x,0)=f(x)$. Injective: if $f(x)+s(y)=0$, applying $g$ gives $y=0$, then $f(x)=0$, so $x=0$. Surjective: for $m\in M$, $g(m-sg(m))=0$, so $m-sg(m)=f(x)$ for some $x$, and $m=\theta(x,g(m))$.

*(c) ⇒ (a) and (b).* Put $r=\pi_1\theta^{-1}$ and $s(y)=\theta(0,y)$. Then $rf(x)=\pi_1\theta^{-1}\theta(x,0)=x$ and $gs(y)=g\theta(0,y)=y$.

*Non-split example.* $0\to\mathbb{Z}\xrightarrow{2}\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/2\to0$. A splitting would give $\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}\oplus\mathbb{Z}/2$, but $\mathbb{Z}$ has no element of order $2$.

### 8.

Let $0\to N'\xrightarrow{f}N\xrightarrow{g}N''$ be exact and write $f_*(\varphi)=f\circ\varphi$, $g_*(\psi)=g\circ\psi$.
- *$f_*$ injective:* if $f\varphi=0$ then $\varphi=0$ since $f$ is injective.
- *$\operatorname{im}f_*\subseteq\ker g_*$:* $g f\varphi=0$ since $gf=0$.
- *$\ker g_*\subseteq\operatorname{im}f_*$:* if $g\psi=0$ then $\psi(M)\subseteq\ker g=f(N')$. Since $f$ is an isomorphism onto its image, $\varphi=f^{-1}\circ\psi:M\to N'$ is a well-defined homomorphism with $f\varphi=\psi$.

*Not exact:* $\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{Z}/2\to0$ is onto, but applying $\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}/2,-)$ gives $0=\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}/2,\mathbb{Z})\to\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbb{Z}/2,\mathbb{Z}/2)\cong\mathbb{Z}/2$, which is not onto.

### 9.

*Lemma.* $X'\xrightarrow{u}X\xrightarrow{v}X''\to0$ is exact iff for every module $P$, $0\to\operatorname{Hom}(X'',P)\xrightarrow{v^*}\operatorname{Hom}(X,P)\xrightarrow{u^*}\operatorname{Hom}(X',P)$ is exact.
*Proof of "⇐":* take $P=X''/\operatorname{im}v$ and the projection $\pi$: $v^*\pi=\pi v=0$, so $\pi=0$ by injectivity of $v^*$, i.e. $v$ is onto. Take $P=X''$: $u^*v^*(\mathrm{id})=vu=0$, so $\operatorname{im}u\subseteq\ker v$. Take $P=X/\operatorname{im}u$ and the projection $q$: $u^*q=0$, so $q=v^*h=hv$ for some $h$, and then $\ker v\subseteq\ker q=\operatorname{im}u$. ("⇒" is the left exactness of $\operatorname{Hom}(-,P)$, proved like Problem 8.)

Now let $N'\to N\to N''\to0$ be exact. For any $P$, the sequence $0\to\operatorname{Hom}(N'',\operatorname{Hom}(M,P))\to\operatorname{Hom}(N,\operatorname{Hom}(M,P))\to\operatorname{Hom}(N',\operatorname{Hom}(M,P))$ is exact (left exactness of $\operatorname{Hom}(-,Q)$). By the natural adjunction $\operatorname{Hom}(N,\operatorname{Hom}(M,P))\cong\operatorname{Hom}(M\otimes N,P)$, this is the sequence $0\to\operatorname{Hom}(M\otimes N'',P)\to\operatorname{Hom}(M\otimes N,P)\to\operatorname{Hom}(M\otimes N',P)$. (Naturality of the adjunction in $N$ is exactly what makes the maps match.) By the lemma, $M\otimes N'\to M\otimes N\to M\otimes N''\to0$ is exact.

*Not left exact:* $0\to\mathbb{Z}\xrightarrow{2}\mathbb{Z}$ is exact, but after $\mathbb{Z}/2\otimes_\mathbb{Z}-$ it becomes $\mathbb{Z}/2\xrightarrow{2=0}\mathbb{Z}/2$, which is not injective.

### 10.

(a) *⇒.* Let $F$ be the free module on the set $P$ and $\pi:F\to P$ the obvious surjection. Since $P$ is projective, $\mathrm{id}_P$ lifts to $s:P\to F$ with $\pi s=\mathrm{id}_P$. By the splitting lemma, $F\cong P\oplus\ker\pi$.

*⇐.* A free module $F$ with basis $(e_i)$ is projective: given a surjection $M\to M''$ and $\varphi:F\to M''$, choose preimages $m_i$ of $\varphi(e_i)$ and define the lift by $e_i\mapsto m_i$. If $F=P\oplus Q$ and $\varphi:P\to M''$, extend by $0$ on $Q$, lift to $\tilde\varphi:F\to M$, and restrict to $P$.

(b) By the Chinese remainder theorem $\mathbb{Z}/6\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\times\mathbb{Z}/3$ as rings, so as a module over itself $\mathbb{Z}/6=3\mathbb{Z}/6\oplus2\mathbb{Z}/6$, where $3\mathbb{Z}/6\cong\mathbb{Z}/2$ as $\mathbb{Z}/6$-modules. So $\mathbb{Z}/2$ is a direct summand of the free module $\mathbb{Z}/6$, hence projective. It is not free: a free $\mathbb{Z}/6$-module of rank $r$ has $6^r$ elements, and $2$ is not a power of $6$.

### 11.

Recall $S^{-1}f(m/s)=f(m)/s$. Since $gf=0$, $(S^{-1}g)(S^{-1}f)=0$. Conversely let $m/s\in S^{-1}M$ with $g(m)/s=0$ in $S^{-1}M''$. Then $t\,g(m)=0$ for some $t\in S$, i.e. $g(tm)=0$, so $tm=f(m')$ for some $m'\in M'$. Hence
$$
\frac ms=\frac{tm}{ts}=\frac{f(m')}{ts}=(S^{-1}f)\left(\frac{m'}{ts}\right).
$$
So the localised sequence is exact.

*Flatness:* if $N'\to N$ is injective, then $0\to N'\to N$ is exact, so $0\to S^{-1}N'\to S^{-1}N$ is exact, i.e. $S^{-1}A\otimes N'\to S^{-1}A\otimes N$ is injective (via the natural isomorphisms $S^{-1}A\otimes_A-\cong S^{-1}(-)$).

### 12.

(a) Suppose $M\ne0$ and let $x_1,\dots,x_n$ ($n\ge1$) be a generating set of minimal size. Since $x_n\in M=\mathfrak{m}M$, write $x_n=\sum_{i=1}^na_ix_i$ with $a_i\in\mathfrak{m}$. Then $(1-a_n)x_n=\sum_{i<n}a_ix_i$. As $a_n\in\mathfrak{m}$, $1-a_n\notin\mathfrak{m}$, so it is a unit in the local ring $A$. Hence $x_n\in(x_1,\dots,x_{n-1})$, contradicting minimality. (If $n=1$, this says $x_1=0$, so $M=0$.)

(b) "Only if" is clear. Conversely, let $N=\sum Ax_i$ and suppose the images span $M/\mathfrak{m}M$; then $N+\mathfrak{m}M=M$. The module $M/N$ is finitely generated and $\mathfrak{m}(M/N)=(\mathfrak{m}M+N)/N=M/N$. By (a), $M/N=0$, i.e. $N=M$.

### 13.

*Nilradical ⊆ every prime:* if $a^n=0\in\mathfrak{p}$ then $a\in\mathfrak{p}$ since $\mathfrak{p}$ is prime.

*Intersection ⊆ nilradical:* suppose $f$ is not nilpotent. Let $\Sigma$ be the set of ideals $I$ with $f^n\notin I$ for all $n\ge0$. $\Sigma\ni(0)$ is non-empty, and the union of a chain in $\Sigma$ is in $\Sigma$, so by Zorn $\Sigma$ has a maximal element $\mathfrak{p}$. It is prime: if $x,y\notin\mathfrak{p}$, then $\mathfrak{p}+(x)$ and $\mathfrak{p}+(y)$ strictly contain $\mathfrak{p}$, so $f^m\in\mathfrak{p}+(x)$ and $f^n\in\mathfrak{p}+(y)$ for some $m,n$. Then $f^{m+n}\in\mathfrak{p}+(xy)$, so $\mathfrak{p}+(xy)\notin\Sigma$, so $xy\notin\mathfrak{p}$. Also $1=f^0\notin\mathfrak{p}$, so $\mathfrak{p}$ is proper. Since $f\notin\mathfrak{p}$, $f$ is not in the intersection of all primes.

### 14.

(a) The prime ideals of the PID $\mathbb{Z}$ are $(0)$ and $(p)$ for primes $p$. Closed sets are $V(n)$ for $n\ge0$ (every ideal is $(n)$). $V(0)$ is everything; $V(1)=\emptyset$; for $n\ge2$, $V(n)=\lbrace(p):p\mid n\rbrace$, a finite set of non-zero primes, and every finite set of non-zero primes arises (take $n$ = their product). So the closed sets are $\emptyset$, the whole space, and the finite sets of non-zero primes. The closed points are the $(p)$ (since $V(p)=\lbrace(p)\rbrace$). The closure of $\lbrace(0)\rbrace$ is $V(0)$, the whole space: $(0)$ is a dense "generic point" and is not closed.

(b) The sets $D(f)$ form a base, so it suffices to show that every cover $\operatorname{Spec}A=\bigcup_{i\in I}D(f_i)$ has a finite subcover. The cover condition says $\bigcap_iV(f_i)=V(\lbrace f_i\rbrace_{i})=V(\mathfrak{a})=\emptyset$, where $\mathfrak{a}$ is the ideal generated by the $f_i$. If $\mathfrak{a}\ne A$, it lies in a maximal ideal, which would be a point of $V(\mathfrak{a})$. So $\mathfrak{a}=A$, and $1=\sum_{j=1}^na_jf_{i_j}$ for finitely many indices. Then $(f_{i_1},\dots,f_{i_n})=A$, so $V(f_{i_1},\dots,f_{i_n})=\emptyset$, i.e. $D(f_{i_1})\cup\cdots\cup D(f_{i_n})=\operatorname{Spec}A$.

### 15.

Suppose $I\subseteq A[x]$ is an ideal that is not finitely generated. Choose $f_1\in I$ of least degree, and inductively $f_{k+1}\in I\setminus(f_1,\dots,f_k)$ of least degree among elements of that set (it is non-empty because $I$ is not finitely generated). Let $n_k=\deg f_k$ and $a_k$ the leading coefficient of $f_k$. By construction $n_1\le n_2\le\cdots$ (an element of $I\setminus(f_1,\dots,f_{k})$ is also in $I\setminus(f_1,\dots,f_{k-1})$).

The chain $(a_1)\subseteq(a_1,a_2)\subseteq\cdots$ in the Noetherian ring $A$ stabilises, so for some $N$, $a_{N+1}=\sum_{i=1}^Nr_ia_i$ with $r_i\in A$. Let
$$
g=f_{N+1}-\sum_{i=1}^Nr_ix^{n_{N+1}-n_i}f_i .
$$
The subtracted sum lies in $(f_1,\dots,f_N)$ and has degree at most $n_{N+1}$ with $x^{n_{N+1}}$-coefficient $a_{N+1}$. So $\deg g<n_{N+1}$, and $g\in I\setminus(f_1,\dots,f_N)$ (else $f_{N+1}\in(f_1,\dots,f_N)$). This contradicts the choice of $f_{N+1}$ as an element of least degree. So every ideal of $A[x]$ is finitely generated.

### 16.

Let $N(a+b\sqrt{-5})=a^2+5b^2=\alpha\bar\alpha$. $N$ is multiplicative, and $N(\alpha)=1$ iff $\alpha=\pm1$ (the units).

(a) $N(2)=4$, $N(3)=9$, $N(1\pm\sqrt{-5})=6$. The equation $a^2+5b^2=2$ or $3$ has no integer solution, so there are no elements of norm $2$ or $3$. If $2=\alpha\beta$ with neither a unit, then $N(\alpha)N(\beta)=4$ with both $>1$, forcing $N(\alpha)=2$: impossible. Similarly for $3$ ($9=3\cdot3$) and $1\pm\sqrt{-5}$ ($6=2\cdot3$). So all four are irreducible. They are not associates of each other ($2$ and $3$ have different norms from each other and from $1\pm\sqrt{-5}$; and $2,3$ are not associates of $1\pm\sqrt{-5}$ as the units are $\pm1$). So $6=2\cdot3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})$ are two essentially different factorisations into irreducibles; $R$ is not a UFD.

(b) Write $\omega=\sqrt{-5}$. Note $R\cong\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^2+5)$.

*Maximality.* $R/\mathfrak{p}\cong\mathbb{Z}[x]/(x^2+5,2,1+x)\cong\mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x^2+1,x+1)$. In $\mathbb{F}_2[x]$, $x^2+1=(x+1)^2$, so this is $\mathbb{F}_2[x]/(x+1)\cong\mathbb{F}_2$, a field. $R/\mathfrak{q}\cong\mathbb{F}_3[x]/(x^2+2,x+1)$; at $x=-1$, $x^2+2=3=0$, so $x+1\mid x^2+2$ and the quotient is $\mathbb{F}_3$. Likewise $R/\mathfrak{q}'\cong\mathbb{F}_3[x]/(x^2+2,x-1)\cong\mathbb{F}_3$. All three ideals are maximal.

*Products.*
- $\mathfrak{p}^2=(4,\ 2+2\omega,\ (1+\omega)^2)=(4,\ 2+2\omega,\ -4+2\omega)$. All generators are divisible by $2$, so $\mathfrak{p}^2\subseteq(2)$. Conversely $(2+2\omega)-(-4+2\omega)=6$ and $6-4=2$ lie in $\mathfrak{p}^2$. So $\mathfrak{p}^2=(2)$.
- $\mathfrak{q}\mathfrak{q}'=(9,\ 3-3\omega,\ 3+3\omega,\ (1+\omega)(1-\omega))=(9,\ 3\pm3\omega,\ 6)$. All divisible by $3$; and $9-6=3$. So $\mathfrak{q}\mathfrak{q}'=(3)$.
- $\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}=(6,\ 2+2\omega,\ 3+3\omega,\ (1+\omega)^2)$. Each generator is divisible by $1+\omega$: $6=(1+\omega)(1-\omega)$, $2+2\omega=2(1+\omega)$, $3+3\omega=3(1+\omega)$. Conversely $(3+3\omega)-(2+2\omega)=1+\omega\in\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}$. So $\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}=(1+\omega)$.
- Applying complex conjugation (an automorphism of $R$ fixing $\mathfrak{p}$, since $1-\omega=2-(1+\omega)\in\mathfrak{p}$, and swapping $\mathfrak{q},\mathfrak{q}'$): $\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}'=(1-\omega)$.

Hence $(6)=(2)(3)=\mathfrak{p}^2\mathfrak{q}\mathfrak{q}'$, and also $(6)=(1+\omega)(1-\omega)=\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}\cdot\mathfrak{p}\mathfrak{q}'$: the same prime factorisation.

(c) If $\mathfrak{p}=(\alpha)$ then $\alpha\mid2$ and $\alpha\mid1+\omega$, so $N(\alpha)$ divides $\gcd(4,6)=2$. There is no element of norm $2$, so $N(\alpha)=1$, $\alpha$ is a unit and $\mathfrak{p}=R$, contradicting $R/\mathfrak{p}\cong\mathbb{F}_2$.

### 17.

Let $K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt d)$ and $\alpha=a+b\sqrt d$ with $a,b\in\mathbb{Q}$.

If $b=0$: $\alpha=a\in\mathbb{Q}$ is integral over $\mathbb{Z}$ iff $a\in\mathbb{Z}$ ($\mathbb{Z}$ is integrally closed, by the rational root test).

If $b\ne0$: the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is $x^2-2ax+(a^2-db^2)$. By the fact quoted, $\alpha$ is integral iff $2a\in\mathbb{Z}$ and $a^2-db^2\in\mathbb{Z}$. (Conversely if both hold, $\alpha$ satisfies a monic integer polynomial.)

Put $A=2a\in\mathbb{Z}$. Then $4(a^2-db^2)=A^2-d(2b)^2\in4\mathbb{Z}$, so $d(2b)^2\in\mathbb{Z}$. Write $2b=p/q$ in lowest terms; then $q^2\mid dp^2$, so $q^2\mid d$, and since $d$ is squarefree, $q=1$. So $B=2b\in\mathbb{Z}$, and the condition is
$$
A^2\equiv dB^2\pmod4 .
$$
Squares mod $4$ are $0$ and $1$.
- If $d\equiv2$ or $3\pmod4$: if $B$ is odd then $A^2\equiv d\pmod4$, impossible. So $B$ is even, then $A^2\equiv0$, so $A$ is even. Hence $a,b\in\mathbb{Z}$, and conversely all of $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d]$ is integral. The ring of integers is $\mathbb{Z}[\sqrt d]$.
- If $d\equiv1\pmod4$: the condition is $A^2\equiv B^2\pmod4$, i.e. $A\equiv B\pmod2$. So the integral elements are $\frac{A+B\sqrt d}2$ with $A\equiv B\pmod2$. These are exactly the elements $m+n\frac{1+\sqrt d}2$ with $m,n\in\mathbb{Z}$ (take $n=B$, $m=(A-B)/2$). So the ring of integers is $\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1+\sqrt d}2\right]$.

### 18.

Let $\mathfrak{m}$ be maximal and $L=\mathbb{C}[x_1,\dots,x_n]/\mathfrak{m}$, a field containing $\mathbb{C}$. As a $\mathbb{C}$-vector space it is spanned by the images of the countably many monomials, so $\dim_\mathbb{C}L$ is countable.

Suppose $t\in L$ is transcendental over $\mathbb{C}$. Then $\mathbb{C}[t]\subseteq L$ is a polynomial ring and $\mathbb{C}(t)\subseteq L$. The elements $\frac1{t-a}$, $a\in\mathbb{C}$, are linearly independent over $\mathbb{C}$: if $\sum_{j=1}^kc_j\frac1{t-a_j}=0$ with distinct $a_j$, multiply by $\prod_j(t-a_j)$ to get $\sum_jc_j\prod_{l\ne j}(t-a_l)=0$ in $\mathbb{C}[t]$; evaluating this polynomial at $t=a_j$ gives $c_j\prod_{l\ne j}(a_j-a_l)=0$, so $c_j=0$. This is an uncountable linearly independent set in a space of countable dimension, a contradiction.

So every element of $L$ is algebraic over $\mathbb{C}$, and as $\mathbb{C}$ is algebraically closed, $L=\mathbb{C}$. Let $a_i\in\mathbb{C}$ be the image of $x_i$. Then $x_i-a_i\in\mathfrak{m}$, so $(x_1-a_1,\dots,x_n-a_n)\subseteq\mathfrak{m}$. The left side is maximal (the quotient is $\mathbb{C}$, via evaluation at $a$), so equality holds.

### 19.

(⇒) Let $V$ be irreducible. For each $g$, $\rho(g)$ commutes with every $\rho(h)$ (as $G$ is abelian), so it is a $G$-map $V\to V$, hence a scalar by Schur's lemma. Then every subspace is invariant, so irreducibility forces $\dim V=1$.

(⇐) If all irreducibles are one-dimensional, the regular representation $\mathbb{C}[G]$ is a direct sum of one-dimensional representations, $\rho_{\mathrm{reg}}(g)$ is simultaneously diagonal in a suitable basis, and so all $\rho_{\mathrm{reg}}(g)$ commute. The regular representation is faithful ($g\cdot1=g\ne1$ for $g\neq1$), so $g\mapsto\rho_{\mathrm{reg}}(g)$ is injective and $G$ is abelian. (Alternatively: $\sum d_i^2=\lvert G\rvert$ with all $d_i=1$ means there are $\lvert G\rvert$ conjugacy classes, so all classes are singletons.)

### 20.

*$D_4=\langle r,s\mid r^4=s^2=1,\ srs^{-1}=r^{-1}\rangle$.* Conjugacy classes: $\lbrace1\rbrace$, $\lbrace r^2\rbrace$, $\lbrace r,r^3\rbrace$, $\lbrace s,sr^2\rbrace$, $\lbrace sr,sr^3\rbrace$: five classes. The commutator subgroup is $\lbrace1,r^2\rbrace$ (it contains $[r,s]=r^2$, and $D_4/\langle r^2\rangle$ has order $4$, hence is abelian). So there are $[G:G']=4$ one-dimensional characters (Problem 25, or directly: homomorphisms to $\lbrace\pm1\rbrace$ determined by the signs of $r$ and $s$), and one more irreducible of degree $d$ with $4+d^2=8$, so $d=2$. Its values follow from column orthogonality against the degree column: $\sum_i\chi_i(1)\chi_i(g)=0$ for $g\ne1$.

*$Q_8=\lbrace\pm1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k\rbrace$.* Conjugacy classes: $\lbrace1\rbrace$, $\lbrace-1\rbrace$, $\lbrace\pm i\rbrace$, $\lbrace\pm j\rbrace$, $\lbrace\pm k\rbrace$. Commutator subgroup $\lbrace\pm1\rbrace$ ($iji^{-1}j^{-1}=-1$), with quotient the Klein four-group. Again four linear characters (determined by signs of $i$ and $j$, with $\chi(k)=\chi(i)\chi(j)$) and one of degree $2$.

Matching classes $1\leftrightarrow1$, $r^2\leftrightarrow-1$, $r\leftrightarrow i$, $s\leftrightarrow j$, $sr\leftrightarrow k$, both tables are:

| | $1$ | $r^2$ / $-1$ | $r$ / $\pm i$ | $s$ / $\pm j$ | $sr$ / $\pm k$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\chi_1$ | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| $\chi_2$ | 1 | 1 | 1 | −1 | −1 |
| $\chi_3$ | 1 | 1 | −1 | 1 | −1 |
| $\chi_4$ | 1 | 1 | −1 | −1 | 1 |
| $\chi_5$ | 2 | −2 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

(The linear characters are trivial on $r^2$, resp. $-1$, since it lies in the commutator subgroup. For $\chi_5$: at $r^2$, $1+1+1+1+2\chi_5=0$ gives $-2$; at the other classes the four linear characters sum to $0$, giving $\chi_5=0$.)

*Not isomorphic.* $D_4$ has five elements of order $2$ ($r^2,s,sr,sr^2,sr^3$), while $Q_8$ has only one ($-1$).

## Qualifying-exam level

### 21.

The ideals $\ker\varphi\subseteq\ker\varphi^2\subseteq\ker\varphi^3\subseteq\cdots$ form an ascending chain, so there is $n$ with $\ker\varphi^n=\ker\varphi^{n+1}$. Let $x\in\ker\varphi$. Since $\varphi^n$ is surjective, $x=\varphi^n(y)$ for some $y$. Then $\varphi^{n+1}(y)=\varphi(x)=0$, so $y\in\ker\varphi^{n+1}=\ker\varphi^n$, so $x=\varphi^n(y)=0$. Hence $\ker\varphi=0$.

### 22.

(a) For $n\ge2$ let $I_n=\lbrace f\in R:f=0\text{ on }[0,1/n]\rbrace$, an ideal. Then $I_2\subseteq I_3\subseteq\cdots$, and the inclusions are strict: the continuous function $f(x)=\max\left(0,x-\frac1{n+1}\right)$ lies in $I_{n+1}$ but not in $I_n$ (it is positive at $x=\frac1n$). An ascending chain that does not stabilise means $R$ is not Noetherian.

(b) *Each $\mathfrak{m}_x$ is maximal:* evaluation $f\mapsto f(x)$ is a surjective ring homomorphism $R\to\mathbb{R}$ with kernel $\mathfrak{m}_x$, and $\mathbb{R}$ is a field.

*Every maximal ideal is some $\mathfrak{m}_x$:* let $\mathfrak{m}$ be maximal and suppose $\mathfrak{m}\ne\mathfrak{m}_x$ for every $x$. Since $\mathfrak{m}_x$ is maximal, $\mathfrak{m}\not\subseteq\mathfrak{m}_x$, so for each $x$ there is $f_x\in\mathfrak{m}$ with $f_x(x)\ne0$, hence $f_x\neq0$ on an open neighbourhood $U_x$ of $x$. By compactness, $[0,1]=U_{x_1}\cup\cdots\cup U_{x_k}$. Then $g=\sum_if_{x_i}^2\in\mathfrak{m}$ and $g>0$ everywhere on $[0,1]$. So $1/g\in R$ and $1=g\cdot(1/g)\in\mathfrak{m}$, a contradiction.

### 23.

*Method 1 (structure).* $\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}[x]/(x^2+1)$. Tensor products commute with quotients: $\mathbb{R}[x]/(f)\otimes_\mathbb{R}\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{C}[x]/(f)$ as rings (by right exactness applied to $0\to(f)\to\mathbb{R}[x]\to\mathbb{R}[x]/(f)\to0$, together with $\mathbb{R}[x]\otimes_\mathbb{R}\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{C}[x]$). So
$$
\mathbb{C}\otimes_\mathbb{R}\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{C}[x]/(x^2+1)=\mathbb{C}[x]/\big((x-i)(x+i)\big)\cong\mathbb{C}[x]/(x-i)\times\mathbb{C}[x]/(x+i)\cong\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C},
$$
using the Chinese remainder theorem ($(x-i)$ and $(x+i)$ are comaximal since their difference $2i$ is a unit).

*Method 2 (explicit).* Define $\Phi:\mathbb{C}\otimes_\mathbb{R}\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}\times\mathbb{C}$ by $\Phi(a\otimes b)=(ab,\bar ab)$, induced by the $\mathbb{R}$-bilinear map $(a,b)\mapsto(ab,\bar ab)$. It is a ring homomorphism (check on pure tensors: $\Phi(aa'\otimes bb')=(aa'bb',\overline{aa'}bb')=\Phi(a\otimes b)\Phi(a'\otimes b')$, and $\Phi(1\otimes1)=(1,1)$). Both sides have real dimension $4$. $\Phi$ is onto: $\Phi(1\otimes1)=(1,1)$ and $\Phi(i\otimes i)=(-1,1)$ give $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ as real combinations, and $\Phi(1\otimes i)=(i,i)$, $\Phi(i\otimes1)=(i,-i)$ give $(i,0),(0,i)$. A surjective linear map between real spaces of the same finite dimension is bijective.

### 24.

Let $W=\operatorname{Res}^G_AV$. Since $A$ is abelian, $W$ is a direct sum of one-dimensional representations of $A$ (Maschke and Problem 19). Pick one, a character $\lambda:A\to\mathbb{C}^\times$ with $\operatorname{Hom}_A(\lambda,W)\ne0$. By Frobenius reciprocity,
$$
\operatorname{Hom}_G(\operatorname{Ind}_A^G\lambda,V)\cong\operatorname{Hom}_A(\lambda,\operatorname{Res}^G_AV)\neq0 .
$$
So there is a non-zero $G$-map $\operatorname{Ind}_A^G\lambda\to V$. Its image is a non-zero subrepresentation of the irreducible $V$, so the map is onto. Hence $\dim V\le\dim\operatorname{Ind}_A^G\lambda=[G:A]\cdot1$.

### 25.

A one-dimensional representation is a homomorphism $\chi:G\to GL_1(\mathbb{C})=\mathbb{C}^\times$. Since $\mathbb{C}^\times$ is abelian, $\chi$ kills every commutator, so it factors uniquely through $G^{ab}=G/G'$; conversely every homomorphism $G/G'\to\mathbb{C}^\times$ pulls back to $G$. Isomorphism classes of one-dimensional representations correspond to the homomorphisms themselves (two one-dimensional representations are isomorphic iff their characters are equal). So the number of one-dimensional representations of $G$ equals the number for the abelian group $G/G'$. For an abelian group $H$, all irreducibles are one-dimensional (Problem 19) and $\sum d_i^2=\lvert H\rvert$, so there are exactly $\lvert H\rvert$ of them. Hence the number is $\lvert G/G'\rvert=[G:G']$.

*$S_4$.* Conjugacy classes correspond to cycle types: $1$, $(12)$, $(12)(34)$, $(123)$, $(1234)$; so there are $5$ irreducibles. The commutator subgroup is $A_4$: $S_4/A_4\cong\mathbb{Z}/2$ is abelian, so $S_4'\subseteq A_4$; and every $3$-cycle is a commutator, e.g. $(12)(13)(12)^{-1}(13)^{-1}=\big((12)(13)\big)^2=(132)^2=(123)$ (composing right to left), and $3$-cycles generate $A_4$. So $[S_4:S_4']=2$: there are two one-dimensional representations (trivial and sign). The other three have degrees $d_3,d_4,d_5\ge2$ with $d_3^2+d_4^2+d_5^2=24-2=22$. Among squares $\ge4$ that are at most $22$ (namely $4,9,16$), the only way to write $22$ as a sum of three is $4+9+9$. So the degrees are $1,1,2,3,3$.
