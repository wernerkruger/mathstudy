These notes condense the module. They follow Folland's order, with Axler's and Tao's viewpoints where those are clearer. Read the texts first; use these notes to consolidate and review. Throughout, $m$ denotes Lebesgue measure and "a.e." means "except on a set of measure zero".

## 1. σ-algebras and measures

**Definition.** A *σ-algebra* on a set $X$ is a collection $\mathcal{M}$ of subsets of $X$ that contains $\emptyset$, is closed under complements, and is closed under countable unions (hence countable intersections). $(X,\mathcal{M})$ is a *measurable space*. For any collection $\mathcal{E}$ of subsets there is a smallest σ-algebra $\sigma(\mathcal{E})$ containing it (the intersection of all σ-algebras containing $\mathcal{E}$). The *Borel σ-algebra* $\mathcal{B}_X$ of a topological space is generated by the open sets. On $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathcal{B}_\mathbb{R}$ is also generated by intervals $(a,b)$, or by rays $(a,\infty)$, or by $(-\infty,a]$ with $a \in \mathbb{Q}$.

**Definition.** A *measure* on $(X,\mathcal{M})$ is a map $\mu:\mathcal{M}\to[0,\infty]$ with $\mu(\emptyset)=0$ and countable additivity: if $E_1,E_2,\dots$ are pairwise disjoint, then $\mu\left(\bigcup E_j\right)=\sum \mu(E_j)$.

**Basic properties.** Monotonicity; countable subadditivity $\mu(\bigcup E_j)\le\sum\mu(E_j)$; *continuity from below*: $E_1\subseteq E_2\subseteq\cdots$ implies $\mu(\bigcup E_j)=\lim\mu(E_j)$; *continuity from above*: $E_1\supseteq E_2\supseteq\cdots$ and $\mu(E_1)<\infty$ imply $\mu(\bigcap E_j)=\lim \mu(E_j)$.

*Proof of continuity from below.* Put $F_1=E_1$, $F_j=E_j\setminus E_{j-1}$. These are disjoint with $\bigcup_{j\le n}F_j=E_n$, so $\mu(\bigcup E_j)=\sum_j \mu(F_j)=\lim_n \mu(E_n)$. For continuity from above, apply this to $E_1\setminus E_j$ and subtract, which needs $\mu(E_1)<\infty$. (Counterexample without it: $E_n=[n,\infty)$, $m(E_n)=\infty$, $\bigcap E_n=\emptyset$.)

**Borel–Cantelli lemma.** If $\sum\mu(E_n)<\infty$ then $\mu(\limsup E_n)=0$, where $\limsup E_n=\bigcap_N\bigcup_{n\ge N}E_n$ is the set of points lying in infinitely many $E_n$. Proof: $\mu(\limsup E_n)\le\mu(\bigcup_{n\ge N}E_n)\le\sum_{n\ge N}\mu(E_n)\to0$.

**Completeness.** $\mu$ is *complete* if every subset of a null set is measurable. Every measure has a completion $\overline{\mu}$ on $\overline{\mathcal{M}}=\lbrace E\cup F: E\in\mathcal{M},\ F\subseteq N\in\mathcal{M},\ \mu(N)=0\rbrace$. Lebesgue measure is the completion of its restriction to Borel sets.

**σ-finiteness.** $\mu$ is σ-finite if $X$ is a countable union of sets of finite measure. Many theorems (Fubini, Radon–Nikodym) need this.

**Examples.** Counting measure on any set; Dirac measure $\delta_x$; Lebesgue measure; on an uncountable set $X$, the σ-algebra of countable/co-countable sets with $\mu(E)=0$ if $E$ is countable and $1$ if co-countable.

## 2. Outer measures and Carathéodory's theorem

**Definition.** An *outer measure* on $X$ is $\mu^*:\mathcal{P}(X)\to[0,\infty]$ with $\mu^*(\emptyset)=0$, monotone, and countably subadditive.

The standard way to get one: given a family $\mathcal{E}$ of "elementary" sets containing $\emptyset$ and $X$ and a function $\rho:\mathcal{E}\to[0,\infty]$ with $\rho(\emptyset)=0$, set
$$
\mu^*(A)=\inf\left\lbrace \sum_{j=1}^\infty \rho(E_j): E_j\in\mathcal{E},\ A\subseteq\bigcup_j E_j\right\rbrace .
$$
For Lebesgue outer measure on $\mathbb{R}^n$, $\mathcal{E}$ is the set of boxes and $\rho$ is volume.

**Definition (Carathéodory).** $A\subseteq X$ is *$\mu^*$-measurable* if for every $E\subseteq X$,
$$
\mu^*(E)=\mu^*(E\cap A)+\mu^*(E\cap A^c).
$$
("$A$ splits every set additively.") By subadditivity only $\ge$ needs checking, and only for $\mu^*(E)<\infty$.

**Theorem (Carathéodory).** The $\mu^*$-measurable sets form a σ-algebra $\mathcal{M}$, and $\mu^*|_{\mathcal{M}}$ is a complete measure.

*Proof sketch.* Closure under complements is built in. For finite unions: if $A,B\in\mathcal{M}$ then for any $E$,
$$
\mu^*(E)=\mu^*(E\cap A)+\mu^*(E\cap A^c\cap B)+\mu^*(E\cap A^c\cap B^c)\ge\mu^*(E\cap(A\cup B))+\mu^*(E\cap(A\cup B)^c),
$$
using subadditivity on $E\cap(A\cup B)=(E\cap A)\cup(E\cap A^c\cap B)$. So $\mathcal{M}$ is an algebra. Now let $A_j\in\mathcal{M}$ be disjoint, $B_n=\bigcup_{j\le n}A_j$, $B=\bigcup_j A_j$. Splitting $E\cap B_n$ by $A_n$ gives inductively $\mu^*(E\cap B_n)=\sum_{j\le n}\mu^*(E\cap A_j)$. Hence
$$
\mu^*(E)=\mu^*(E\cap B_n)+\mu^*(E\cap B_n^c)\ge\sum_{j\le n}\mu^*(E\cap A_j)+\mu^*(E\cap B^c).
$$
Let $n\to\infty$ and use subadditivity: $\mu^*(E)\ge\sum_j\mu^*(E\cap A_j)+\mu^*(E\cap B^c)\ge\mu^*(E\cap B)+\mu^*(E\cap B^c)$. So $B\in\mathcal{M}$, and all inequalities are equalities; taking $E=B$ gives countable additivity. Completeness: if $\mu^*(N)=0$ then $\mu^*(E\cap N)+\mu^*(E\cap N^c)\le 0+\mu^*(E)$. $\square$

**Extension theorem (Carathéodory–Hahn).** If $\mu_0$ is a *premeasure* on an algebra $\mathcal{A}$ (finitely additive, and countably additive whenever a countable disjoint union in $\mathcal{A}$ happens to lie in $\mathcal{A}$), the induced outer measure $\mu^*$ restricts to a measure on $\sigma(\mathcal{A})$ extending $\mu_0$. If $\mu_0$ is σ-finite the extension is unique.

The hard step for Lebesgue measure is showing that box volume is a premeasure, i.e. that a box cannot be covered by countably many boxes of smaller total volume. This uses compactness (Heine–Borel): shrink the box slightly to a compact one and enlarge the covering boxes slightly to open ones; finitely many suffice, and the finite case is combinatorial.

**Lebesgue–Stieltjes measures.** For $F:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ increasing and right-continuous, $\mu_F((a,b])=F(b)-F(a)$ extends to a unique Borel measure. Every Borel measure on $\mathbb{R}$ finite on bounded sets arises this way. $F(x)=x$ gives Lebesgue measure; a step function gives a sum of point masses; the Cantor function gives a continuous singular measure.

## 3. Lebesgue measure: regularity, invariance, non-measurable sets

**Regularity.** For Lebesgue measurable $E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$:
$$
m(E)=\inf\lbrace m(U): U\supseteq E \text{ open}\rbrace=\sup\lbrace m(K):K\subseteq E\text{ compact}\rbrace.
$$
Equivalently, $E$ is measurable iff for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is open $U\supseteq E$ with $m^*(U\setminus E)<\varepsilon$; iff $E=G\setminus N$ with $G$ a $G_\delta$ set and $m(N)=0$; iff $E=F\cup N$ with $F$ an $F_\sigma$ set and $m(N)=0$.

**Invariance.** $m(E+x)=m(E)$ and $m(TE)=\lvert\det T\rvert\, m(E)$ for linear $T$. Up to a constant, Lebesgue measure is the only translation-invariant Borel measure on $\mathbb{R}^n$ that is finite on compact sets (a first case of uniqueness of Haar measure).

**The Cantor set.** $C=\bigcap_k C_k$ where $C_k$ is the union of $2^k$ closed intervals of length $3^{-k}$. Then $m(C)\le (2/3)^k\to0$, but $C$ is uncountable: it consists of the points of $[0,1]$ with a ternary expansion using only digits $0$ and $2$, and that set is in bijection with $\lbrace 0,1\rbrace^\mathbb{N}$. Variations ("fat Cantor sets") remove smaller middle intervals and give closed nowhere dense sets of positive measure.

**The Cantor function** $F:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ is continuous, increasing, constant on each removed interval (so $F'=0$ a.e.), and $F(0)=0$, $F(1)=1$. So $\int_0^1F'\,dx=0\neq F(1)-F(0)$: the fundamental theorem of calculus fails for merely continuous increasing functions.

**Vitali's non-measurable set.** Define $x\sim y$ on $[0,1]$ iff $x-y\in\mathbb{Q}$. Using the axiom of choice, pick one point from each class to get $V$. For the rationals $q_1,q_2,\dots$ in $[-1,1]$ the translates $V+q_k$ are pairwise disjoint and
$$
[0,1]\subseteq\bigcup_k(V+q_k)\subseteq[-1,2].
$$
If $V$ were measurable, translation invariance and countable additivity would give $1\le\sum_k m(V)\le 3$, impossible whether $m(V)=0$ or $m(V)>0$. So there is no translation-invariant countably additive measure defined on all subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ that gives intervals their length. (Solovay showed that without choice it is consistent that every subset of $\mathbb{R}$ is Lebesgue measurable.)

**Also useful:** every set of positive outer measure contains a non-measurable subset; and the Lebesgue σ-algebra is strictly larger than the Borel σ-algebra (count: there are $2^{\mathfrak{c}}$ subsets of the Cantor set, all Lebesgue measurable, but only $\mathfrak{c}$ Borel sets).

## 4. Measurable functions

**Definition.** $f:(X,\mathcal{M})\to(Y,\mathcal{N})$ is measurable if $f^{-1}(B)\in\mathcal{M}$ for every $B\in\mathcal{N}$. It suffices to check this for $B$ in a generating family. For $f:X\to[-\infty,\infty]$ it suffices that $\lbrace f>a\rbrace\in\mathcal{M}$ for every $a\in\mathbb{R}$.

**Closure properties.** Sums, products, compositions with continuous functions, $\sup_n f_n$, $\inf_n f_n$, $\limsup f_n$, $\liminf f_n$, and pointwise limits of measurable functions are measurable.

*Pitfall.* "Lebesgue measurable" functions $\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ means: preimages of Borel sets are Lebesgue measurable. Then $f\circ g$ with both Lebesgue measurable need **not** be measurable. Compositions are safe if the outer function is Borel.

**Simple approximation.** If $f:X\to[0,\infty]$ is measurable, there are simple functions $0\le\varphi_1\le\varphi_2\le\cdots\le f$ with $\varphi_n\to f$ pointwise, and uniformly on any set where $f$ is bounded. Explicitly,
$$
\varphi_n=\sum_{k=0}^{n2^n-1}\frac{k}{2^n}\chi_{\lbrace k2^{-n}\le f<(k+1)2^{-n}\rbrace}+n\,\chi_{\lbrace f\ge n\rbrace}.
$$

**Littlewood's three principles** (Lebesgue measure, finite-measure sets): every measurable set is nearly a finite union of intervals; every measurable function is nearly continuous (**Lusin**: for $\varepsilon>0$ there is a closed $F$ with $m(E\setminus F)<\varepsilon$ and $f|_F$ continuous); every convergent sequence is nearly uniformly convergent (**Egorov**).

**Egorov's theorem.** If $\mu(X)<\infty$ and $f_n\to f$ a.e. (all finite-valued and measurable), then for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $E$ with $\mu(E)<\varepsilon$ and $f_n\to f$ uniformly on $X\setminus E$.

*Proof.* Let $E_{n}(k)=\bigcup_{j\ge n}\lbrace \lvert f_j-f\rvert\ge1/k\rbrace$. For fixed $k$ these decrease in $n$ to a null set, so by continuity from above (finite measure!) pick $n_k$ with $\mu(E_{n_k}(k))<\varepsilon2^{-k}$. Put $E=\bigcup_kE_{n_k}(k)$. Off $E$, $\lvert f_j-f\rvert<1/k$ for all $j\ge n_k$. $\square$

## 5. The integral and the convergence theorems

**Construction.** For simple $\varphi=\sum a_j\chi_{E_j}\ge0$ (canonical form), $\int\varphi\,d\mu=\sum a_j\mu(E_j)$ with $0\cdot\infty=0$. For measurable $f\ge0$,
$$
\int f\,d\mu=\sup\left\lbrace\int\varphi\,d\mu:0\le\varphi\le f,\ \varphi\text{ simple}\right\rbrace.
$$
(Axler instead defines it as a supremum over finite measurable partitions of $\sum_j \mu(A_j)\inf_{A_j} f$, which is equivalent.) For real $f$, write $f=f^+-f^-$; $f$ is *integrable* ($f\in L^1$) if $\int\lvert f\rvert<\infty$, and then $\int f=\int f^+-\int f^-$. Complex $f$: integrate real and imaginary parts.

**Monotone convergence theorem (MCT).** If $0\le f_1\le f_2\le\cdots$ are measurable and $f_n\to f$, then $\int f_n\to\int f$.

*Proof.* $\int f_n$ increases to some $L\le\int f$. For the reverse, fix simple $0\le\varphi\le f$ and $0<c<1$. The sets $E_n=\lbrace f_n\ge c\varphi\rbrace$ increase to $X$ (if $\varphi(x)>0$ then $f_n(x)\to f(x)>c\varphi(x)$; if $\varphi(x)=0$ then $x\in E_n$ for all $n$). So $\int f_n\ge c\int_{E_n}\varphi\to c\int\varphi$, using continuity from below of the measure $E\mapsto\int_E\varphi$. Let $c\to1$ and take the sup over $\varphi$. $\square$

**Corollaries.** Integrals of non-negative functions are additive; $\int\sum f_n=\sum\int f_n$ for $f_n\ge0$; $E\mapsto\int_Ef\,d\mu$ is a measure for $f\ge0$.

**Fatou's lemma.** If $f_n\ge0$ are measurable, $\int\liminf f_n\le\liminf\int f_n$.

*Proof.* $g_k=\inf_{n\ge k}f_n$ increases to $\liminf f_n$ and $\int g_k\le\int f_n$ for $n\ge k$; apply MCT. $\square$

Strict inequality can occur: $f_n=\chi_{[n,n+1]}$ ("escape to horizontal infinity"), $f_n=n\chi_{(0,1/n)}$ ("escape to vertical infinity"), $f_n=\frac1n\chi_{[0,n]}$ ("escape to width infinity"). In each, $f_n\to0$ pointwise but $\int f_n=1$. These three examples are worth memorising: every failure of an interchange of limit and integral looks like one of them.

**Dominated convergence theorem (DCT).** If $f_n\to f$ a.e. and $\lvert f_n\rvert\le g$ with $g\in L^1$, then $f\in L^1$ and $\int\lvert f_n-f\rvert\to0$; in particular $\int f_n\to\int f$.

*Proof.* $2g-\lvert f_n-f\rvert\ge0$; Fatou gives $\int2g\le\liminf\int(2g-\lvert f_n-f\rvert)=\int 2g-\limsup\int\lvert f_n-f\rvert$. Since $\int2g<\infty$, $\limsup\int\lvert f_n-f\rvert\le0$. $\square$

**Standard consequences.**
- *Differentiation under the integral:* if $f(\cdot,t)\in L^1$, $\partial_tf$ exists and $\lvert\partial_tf(x,t)\rvert\le g(x)\in L^1$ for all $t$ near $t_0$, then $\frac{d}{dt}\int f(x,t)\,dx=\int\partial_tf(x,t)\,dx$ (mean value theorem plus DCT).
- *Riemann vs Lebesgue:* a bounded $f$ on $[a,b]$ is Riemann integrable iff it is continuous a.e. (Lebesgue's criterion), and then the two integrals agree. Improper Riemann integrals such as $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx$ can converge without $f$ being Lebesgue integrable.
- *$L^1$ as a space:* identify functions equal a.e. Then $\lVert f\rVert_1=\int\lvert f\rvert$ is a norm.

## 6. Modes of convergence

On a measure space, $f_n\to f$:
- **a.e.** if $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ off a null set;
- **in measure** if $\mu(\lbrace\lvert f_n-f\rvert\ge\varepsilon\rbrace)\to0$ for every $\varepsilon>0$;
- **in $L^p$** if $\lVert f_n-f\rVert_p\to0$;
- **almost uniformly** if for every $\varepsilon$ there is $E$, $\mu(E)<\varepsilon$, with uniform convergence off $E$.

**Implications.**
- $L^p$ convergence $\Rightarrow$ convergence in measure (Chebyshev: $\mu(\lvert f_n-f\rvert\ge\varepsilon)\le\varepsilon^{-p}\lVert f_n-f\rVert_p^p$).
- Almost uniform $\Rightarrow$ a.e. and in measure.
- On finite measure spaces, a.e. $\Rightarrow$ almost uniform (Egorov) $\Rightarrow$ in measure.
- **Riesz:** convergence in measure $\Rightarrow$ some subsequence converges a.e. (choose $n_k$ with $\mu(\lvert f_{n_k}-f\rvert\ge2^{-k})<2^{-k}$ and use Borel–Cantelli).
- DCT upgrades a.e. (or in-measure) convergence to $L^1$ convergence.

**Counterexamples.**
- The *typewriter sequence* $\chi_{[j2^{-k},(j+1)2^{-k}]}$ (enumerated over $k$, then $j=0,\dots,2^k-1$) tends to $0$ in $L^1[0,1]$ and in measure but converges at no point.
- The three escape examples above converge a.e. but not in $L^1$; $\chi_{[n,n+1]}$ does not even converge in measure (infinite measure space).

## 7. Product measures: Tonelli and Fubini

Given $(X,\mathcal{M},\mu)$ and $(Y,\mathcal{N},\nu)$, the product σ-algebra $\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}$ is generated by rectangles $A\times B$. The premeasure $\pi(A\times B)=\mu(A)\nu(B)$ on finite disjoint unions of rectangles extends to a measure $\mu\times\nu$; it is unique when $\mu,\nu$ are σ-finite.

**Monotone class lemma.** If an algebra $\mathcal{A}$ is contained in a monotone class $\mathcal{C}$ (closed under increasing unions and decreasing intersections), then $\sigma(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{C}$. (Alternatively, Dynkin's π-λ theorem.) This is how one shows that a property true for rectangles is true for all of $\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}$: for example, that $x\mapsto\nu(E_x)$ is measurable and $(\mu\times\nu)(E)=\int\nu(E_x)\,d\mu(x)$, where $E_x=\lbrace y:(x,y)\in E\rbrace$.

**Tonelli's theorem.** If $\mu,\nu$ are σ-finite and $f\ge0$ is $\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}$-measurable, then $x\mapsto\int f(x,y)\,d\nu(y)$ and $y\mapsto\int f(x,y)\,d\mu(x)$ are measurable and
$$
\int_{X\times Y}f\,d(\mu\times\nu)=\int_X\left(\int_Yf(x,y)\,d\nu(y)\right)d\mu(x)=\int_Y\left(\int_Xf(x,y)\,d\mu(x)\right)d\nu(y).
$$

**Fubini's theorem.** Same conclusion if $f\in L^1(\mu\times\nu)$ (with the inner integrals defined a.e. and integrable).

**How to use them.** To justify swapping the order for a signed $f$: apply Tonelli to $\lvert f\rvert$ (always allowed for σ-finite spaces), show one iterated integral of $\lvert f\rvert$ is finite, then apply Fubini to $f$.

**Counterexamples.**
- *Not integrable:* on $[0,1]^2$, $f(x,y)=\dfrac{x^2-y^2}{(x^2+y^2)^2}$ has iterated integrals $\pi/4$ and $-\pi/4$.
- *Not σ-finite:* $\mu$ = Lebesgue on $[0,1]$, $\nu$ = counting measure on $[0,1]$, $f=\chi_{\Delta}$ with $\Delta$ the diagonal. Then $\int\!\int f\,d\mu\,d\nu=0$ but $\int\!\int f\,d\nu\,d\mu=1$.

**Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.** $m^{n+k}$ is the completion of $m^n\times m^k$. The completion has a subtlety: for Fubini–Tonelli with complete measures, the slice functions are only measurable for a.e. $x$. Change of variables: for a $C^1$ diffeomorphism $G$ between open sets, $\int_{G(\Omega)}f=\int_\Omega(f\circ G)\lvert\det DG\rvert$. Polar coordinates: $\int_{\mathbb{R}^n}f=\int_0^\infty\int_{S^{n-1}}f(r\omega)r^{n-1}\,d\sigma(\omega)\,dr$.

## 8. $L^p$ spaces

For $1\le p<\infty$, $\lVert f\rVert_p=\left(\int\lvert f\rvert^p\right)^{1/p}$; $\lVert f\rVert_\infty=\inf\lbrace M:\lvert f\rvert\le M\text{ a.e.}\rbrace$ (the essential supremum). $L^p$ is the space of equivalence classes (equal a.e.) with finite norm. Exponents $p,q$ are *conjugate* if $1/p+1/q=1$ (with $1$ and $\infty$ conjugate).

**Young's inequality.** For $a,b\ge0$, $ab\le\frac{a^p}{p}+\frac{b^q}{q}$ (concavity of $\log$).

**Hölder's inequality.** $\lVert fg\rVert_1\le\lVert f\rVert_p\lVert g\rVert_q$.

*Proof.* Trivial if either norm is $0$ or $\infty$. Otherwise normalise to $\lVert f\rVert_p=\lVert g\rVert_q=1$ and integrate Young's inequality: $\int\lvert fg\rvert\le\frac1p+\frac1q=1$. Equality iff $\lvert f\rvert^p$ and $\lvert g\rvert^q$ are proportional a.e. $\square$

**Minkowski's inequality.** $\lVert f+g\rVert_p\le\lVert f\rVert_p+\lVert g\rVert_p$.

*Proof ($1<p<\infty$).* $\lvert f+g\rvert^p\le(\lvert f\rvert+\lvert g\rvert)\lvert f+g\rvert^{p-1}$. Apply Hölder with exponents $p$ and $q=p/(p-1)$ to each part: $\int\lvert f+g\rvert^p\le(\lVert f\rVert_p+\lVert g\rVert_p)\lVert f+g\rVert_p^{p-1}$. Divide (after checking $\lVert f+g\rVert_p<\infty$ via $\lvert f+g\rvert^p\le2^{p-1}(\lvert f\rvert^p+\lvert g\rvert^p)$). $\square$

**Riesz–Fischer theorem.** $L^p$ is complete for $1\le p\le\infty$. Moreover, every $L^p$-convergent sequence ($p<\infty$) has an a.e.-convergent subsequence.

*Proof ($p<\infty$).* A normed space is complete iff every absolutely convergent series converges. Let $\sum\lVert f_k\rVert_p=B<\infty$. Put $G_n=\sum_{k\le n}\lvert f_k\rvert$ and $G=\sum_k\lvert f_k\rvert$. Minkowski gives $\lVert G_n\rVert_p\le B$, and MCT gives $\lVert G\rVert_p\le B$, so $G<\infty$ a.e. Hence $F=\sum f_k$ converges absolutely a.e., $\lvert F\rvert\le G\in L^p$, and $\lvert F-\sum_{k\le n}f_k\rvert^p\le(2G)^p\in L^1$. By DCT, $\sum_{k\le n} f_k\to F$ in $L^p$. $\square$

**Inclusions.** If $\mu(X)<\infty$ and $p<q$, then $L^q\subseteq L^p$ with $\lVert f\rVert_p\le\mu(X)^{1/p-1/q}\lVert f\rVert_q$ (Hölder with $\lvert f\rvert^p\cdot1$). On $\ell^p$ (counting measure) the inclusions reverse: $\ell^p\subseteq\ell^q$ for $p<q$. On $\mathbb{R}$, neither inclusion holds.

**Density.** Simple functions are dense in $L^p$ for $p<\infty$. On $\mathbb{R}^n$, $C_c(\mathbb{R}^n)$ is dense in $L^p$ for $p<\infty$ (by regularity and Urysohn), and so are step functions (finite combinations of indicators of boxes). Consequently translation is continuous: $\lVert f(\cdot+h)-f\rVert_p\to0$ as $h\to0$ for $p<\infty$. This fails for $p=\infty$ (take $f=\chi_{[0,1]}$).

**Duality (preview of 3.2).** For σ-finite $\mu$ and $1\le p<\infty$, the map $g\mapsto(f\mapsto\int fg)$ is an isometric isomorphism $L^q\to(L^p)^*$. The proof uses Radon–Nikodym. For $p=\infty$ it is not onto in general.

## 9. Signed measures and the Radon–Nikodym theorem

A *signed measure* $\nu$ on $(X,\mathcal{M})$ is countably additive, takes values in $(-\infty,\infty]$ or $[-\infty,\infty)$, and $\nu(\emptyset)=0$. A set $P$ is *positive* if $\nu(E)\ge0$ for all measurable $E\subseteq P$.

**Hahn decomposition.** There is a partition $X=P\cup N$ with $P$ positive and $N$ negative; it is unique up to null sets.

*Proof idea.* Assume $\nu$ omits $+\infty$. Let $s=\sup\lbrace\nu(E):E\text{ positive}\rbrace$, attained by a union $P$ of a maximising sequence. If $N=P^c$ contained a set of positive measure, a "greedy" lemma (every set of positive finite measure contains a positive subset of positive measure, found by repeatedly removing subsets of the most negative available measure) would let you enlarge $P$. $\square$

**Jordan decomposition.** $\nu=\nu^+-\nu^-$ with $\nu^+(E)=\nu(E\cap P)$, $\nu^-(E)=-\nu(E\cap N)$; these are mutually singular, and the decomposition is unique among such pairs. The *total variation* is $\lvert\nu\rvert=\nu^++\nu^-$.

**Definitions.** $\nu\ll\mu$ (*absolutely continuous*) if $\mu(E)=0\Rightarrow\nu(E)=0$. $\nu\perp\mu$ (*mutually singular*) if $X=A\cup B$ disjoint with $\mu(A)=0=\nu(B)$.

For finite $\nu$, $\nu\ll\mu$ is equivalent to the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ condition: for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\delta>0$ with $\mu(E)<\delta\Rightarrow\lvert\nu(E)\rvert<\varepsilon$. (The proof of "$\Rightarrow$" uses Borel–Cantelli. Finiteness matters: $\nu(E)=\int_E x^{-1}\,dx$ on $(0,1]$ is absolutely continuous but fails the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ condition.)

**Lebesgue–Radon–Nikodym theorem.** Let $\mu,\nu$ be σ-finite (positive) measures. Then $\nu=\nu_a+\nu_s$ uniquely with $\nu_a\ll\mu$ and $\nu_s\perp\mu$, and there is a measurable $f\ge0$, unique $\mu$-a.e., with $\nu_a(E)=\int_Ef\,d\mu$. If $\nu\ll\mu$ then $\nu=f\,d\mu$; $f=\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}$ is the *Radon–Nikodym derivative*.

*Von Neumann's proof (finite measures).* Put $\rho=\mu+\nu$. The functional $\Lambda(g)=\int g\,d\nu$ satisfies $\lvert\Lambda(g)\rvert\le\nu(X)^{1/2}\lVert g\rVert_{L^2(\rho)}$, so by the Riesz representation theorem for the Hilbert space $L^2(\rho)$ there is $h\in L^2(\rho)$ with $\int g\,d\nu=\int gh\,d\rho$ for all $g\in L^2(\rho)$. Testing with indicators shows $0\le h\le1$ $\rho$-a.e. Rearranging, $\int g(1-h)\,d\nu=\int gh\,d\mu$. Let $A=\lbrace h<1\rbrace$, $B=\lbrace h=1\rbrace$. Taking $g=\chi_B$ gives $\mu(B)=0$, so $\nu_s=\nu|_B\perp\mu$. On $A$, take $g=(1+h+\dots+h^{n})\chi_E$ and let $n\to\infty$ by MCT: $\nu(E\cap A)=\int_E\frac{h}{1-h}\chi_A\,d\mu$. So $f=\frac{h}{1-h}\chi_A$. The σ-finite case follows by partitioning $X$. $\square$

**Failure without σ-finiteness.** Lebesgue measure $m$ on $[0,1]$ is absolutely continuous with respect to counting measure $c$, but $m=f\,dc$ would force $f(x)=m(\lbrace x\rbrace)=0$ for all $x$.

**Chain rule.** If $\lambda\ll\nu\ll\mu$ then $\frac{d\lambda}{d\mu}=\frac{d\lambda}{d\nu}\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}$ a.e. Probabilists know $\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}$ as a likelihood ratio and as the foundation of conditional expectation.

**Complex measures** have $\lvert\nu\rvert(E)=\sup\sum_j\lvert\nu(E_j)\rvert$ over measurable partitions; $\lvert\nu\rvert$ is a finite measure, and $d\nu=h\,d\lvert\nu\rvert$ with $\lvert h\rvert=1$.

## 10. Differentiation on $\mathbb{R}^n$

Question: for $f\in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^n)$, does $\frac{1}{m(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}f\to f(x)$ as $r\to0$?

**Vitali covering lemma (finite version).** Any finite family of balls has a disjoint subfamily $B_1,\dots,B_k$ with $\bigcup(\text{all})\subseteq\bigcup 3B_j$, where $3B$ is the concentric ball of three times the radius. (Greedy: take the largest ball, discard all balls meeting it, repeat.)

**Hardy–Littlewood maximal function.** $Hf(x)=\sup_{r>0}\frac1{m(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}\lvert f\rvert$. It is lower semicontinuous and satisfies the *weak (1,1) inequality*:
$$
m(\lbrace Hf>\alpha\rbrace)\le\frac{3^n}{\alpha}\lVert f\rVert_1.
$$
*Proof.* Let $K\subseteq\lbrace Hf>\alpha\rbrace$ be compact. Each $x\in K$ has a ball $B_x$ with $\int_{B_x}\lvert f\rvert>\alpha m(B_x)$. Take a finite subcover, then a Vitali disjoint subfamily: $m(K)\le3^n\sum m(B_j)\le\frac{3^n}{\alpha}\sum\int_{B_j}\lvert f\rvert\le\frac{3^n}{\alpha}\lVert f\rVert_1$. Use inner regularity. $\square$

($Hf$ is never in $L^1$ unless $f=0$, but $H$ is bounded on $L^p$ for $1<p\le\infty$.)

**Lebesgue differentiation theorem.** If $f\in L^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^n)$, then for a.e. $x$,
$$
\lim_{r\to0}\frac{1}{m(B(x,r))}\int_{B(x,r)}\lvert f(y)-f(x)\rvert\,dy=0.
$$
Such $x$ are *Lebesgue points*.

*Proof.* Localise to $f\in L^1$. For continuous $g$ the limit is $0$ everywhere. Given $\varepsilon$, choose $g\in C_c$ with $\lVert f-g\rVert_1<\varepsilon$. With $h=f-g$, the $\limsup$ of the averages is at most $Hh(x)+\lvert h(x)\rvert$. So
$$
m\left(\left\lbrace\limsup>2\alpha\right\rbrace\right)\le m(\lbrace Hh>\alpha\rbrace)+m(\lbrace\lvert h\rvert>\alpha\rbrace)\le\frac{3^n\varepsilon}{\alpha}+\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}.
$$
Let $\varepsilon\to0$, then take the union over $\alpha=1/k$. $\square$

**Consequences.**
- *Density points:* for measurable $E$, a.e. $x\in E$ satisfies $m(E\cap B(x,r))/m(B(x,r))\to1$. A measurable set cannot be "half of every interval".
- *FTC, part 1:* if $f\in L^1[a,b]$ and $F(x)=\int_a^xf$, then $F'=f$ a.e.

**Functions of one variable.**
- *Lebesgue's theorem:* monotone functions, and hence functions of bounded variation (differences of increasing functions), are differentiable a.e.
- $F:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}$ is *absolutely continuous* (AC) if for every $\varepsilon$ there is $\delta$ such that $\sum\lvert F(b_i)-F(a_i)\rvert<\varepsilon$ whenever $(a_i,b_i)$ are disjoint with $\sum(b_i-a_i)<\delta$. Lipschitz $\Rightarrow$ AC $\Rightarrow$ BV and uniformly continuous.
- **FTC for Lebesgue integrals:** $F$ is AC on $[a,b]$ iff $F'$ exists a.e., $F'\in L^1$, and $F(x)-F(a)=\int_a^xF'$ for all $x$.
- In measure language: for increasing right-continuous $F$, the decomposition of $\mu_F$ into absolutely continuous, singular continuous and pure point parts corresponds to $F=F_{ac}+F_{sc}+F_{pp}$; $F_{ac}$ is the integral of $F'$. The Cantor function is purely singular continuous.

## 11. Common pitfalls

1. **Using DCT without a dominating function that is independent of $n$.** The bound must be a single $g\in L^1$. If you cannot find one, try Fatou, MCT, uniform integrability, or a truncation.
2. **Using continuity from above on infinite-measure sets.**
3. **Swapping integrals without checking Tonelli on $\lvert f\rvert$ first.**
4. **Confusing "$f_n\to f$ a.e." with "$\lVert f_n-f\rVert_\infty\to0$".** The latter is uniform convergence off a single null set.
5. **Forgetting that $L^p$ elements are equivalence classes.** "$f(0)$" is meaningless for $f\in L^p$; "$f$ is continuous" means "has a continuous representative".
6. **Assuming Lebesgue measurable sets are Borel**, or that the composition of Lebesgue measurable functions is measurable.
7. **Treating improper Riemann integrals as Lebesgue integrals.** $\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx$ exists as an improper integral, but $\frac{\sin x}{x}\notin L^1(0,\infty)$.
8. **Applying Radon–Nikodym to non-σ-finite measures**, or forgetting that the derivative is only unique a.e.

## 12. Connections forward

- **[3.2 Functional analysis](../3.2-functional-analysis/):** $L^p$ spaces are the basic Banach spaces, and $L^2$ is the basic Hilbert space. Duality $(L^p)^*=L^q$, weak convergence in $L^p$, and compactness of integral operators all rest on this module. The Riesz–Markov theorem identifies $C(K)^*$ with finite regular Borel measures (Folland ch. 7).
- **Probability:** a probability space is a measure space of total mass 1; random variables are measurable functions; expectation is the integral; conditional expectation is a Radon–Nikodym derivative; the Borel–Cantelli lemma and modes of convergence are used constantly. Kolmogorov's extension theorem is a Carathéodory construction on an infinite product.
- **Harmonic analysis and PDE:** the maximal function and Lebesgue differentiation are the start of Calderón–Zygmund theory; $L^p$ and Fubini underlie convolution, Fourier transforms and Sobolev spaces.
- **Geometry:** Hausdorff measure (Stein–Shakarchi ch. 7, Folland §11.2) defines fractal dimension. Haar measure on locally compact groups generalises Lebesgue measure and is needed in representation theory of compact groups (compare [3.3](../3.3-graduate-algebra/), where averaging over a finite group proves Maschke's theorem).
- **[3.5 Smooth manifolds](../3.5-smooth-manifolds/):** integration of differential forms reduces, in charts, to Lebesgue integrals on $\mathbb{R}^n$ and the change-of-variables formula; Sard's theorem is a measure-zero statement.
